# Healthcare Marketing Playbook

## Executive brief

Healthcare marketing is not one market with unusually strict copy review. It is a set of linked markets in which the person exposed to a campaign may not be the prescriber, purchaser, payer, user, or caregiver—and in which a poorly framed claim can change a clinical decision. The practical consequence is that strategy must begin with a stakeholder-and-evidence map, not a channel plan. The American College of Healthcare Executives’ healthcare-marketing framework explicitly centers physicians, patients, payers, the public, and politics rather than relying only on the classic four Ps ([ACHE](https://www.ache.org/learning-center/publications/books/2249i)).

This playbook treats marketing compliance as a design constraint. It assumes a broader regulated-markets program already covers generic review workflow, platform terms, and endorsement law. Here the emphasis is healthcare-specific: protected health information (PHI) in acquisition funnels; FDA-regulated indications and risk presentation; medical/commercial boundaries; inducements, samples, and transfers of value; Medicare communications; and the evidentiary line between wellness, structure/function, and disease claims.

The central operating model is:

1. **Choose the stakeholder and behavior.** Define whether the campaign should create patient demand, change an HCP’s knowledge, earn formulary access, activate an employer buyer, enable a caregiver, or generate a referral.
2. **Define the claim universe.** For drugs, anchor to the approved labeling; for devices, clearance/approval and instructions for use (IFU); for supplements, the structure/function boundary and substantiation file; for services, credentialed clinical evidence and actual operational capacity.
3. **Engineer privacy before media.** Inventory every event, identifier, audience, destination, vendor, and contract before placing a tag on a health-related property. OCR’s tracking-technology guidance says regulated entities may not impermissibly disclose PHI to tracking vendors and describes when a business associate agreement (BAA) is required ([HHS OCR](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/hipaa-online-tracking/index.html)).
4. **Route through the right governance lane.** Commercial promotion, medical/scientific exchange, public relations, patient support, and disease education need different owners, claims, and metrics.
5. **Measure downstream healthcare behavior, not merely clicks.** Use qualified appointments, referral completion, starts, adherence, incremental prescriptions, payer access, or service-line contribution margin, with privacy-preserving linkage and experimental controls.

## 1. Why healthcare marketing is uniquely hard

### Safety and information asymmetry

The audience often cannot independently evaluate a product’s benefit-risk profile, diagnostic suitability, or evidentiary quality. FDA therefore evaluates prescription-drug promotion not only for literal falsity but also for omitted or minimized risk, misleading presentation, and lack of fair balance; its sample “correct” product-claim ad shows benefits paired with prominent risk information ([FDA product-claim example](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/prescription-drug-advertising/product-claim-ad-correct)). FDA’s consumer explainer likewise distinguishes product-claim, reminder, and help-seeking advertising because the inclusion of a product and indication changes the regulatory obligations ([FDA prescription drug advertising](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/information-consumers-and-patients-drugs/prescription-drug-advertising)).

Healthcare claims also decay differently from ordinary product claims. A new safety signal, label update, payer restriction, clinical guideline, or standard-of-care change can make an approved asset commercially misleading even before its planned expiration. Every reusable claim therefore needs an owner, evidence source, audience, approval date, and refresh trigger.

### Third-party payment and split incentives

The consumer may want the therapy, the clinician may decide whether it is clinically appropriate, the payer may impose utilization management, and an employer may select the plan. Caregivers can shape adherence and site-of-care choice without appearing in claims data as the treated patient. This produces different value stories: symptom relief for patients; evidence, workflow, and patient selection for HCPs; total cost and outcomes for payers; absenteeism and access for employers; navigation and burden reduction for caregivers.

Healthcare marketers should therefore resist a single “customer journey.” A digital-health product may have five journeys: consumer discovery, clinician recommendation, security/procurement review, benefit-design approval, and ongoing engagement. FDA’s patient-focused drug-development program reinforces that patient experience is distinct evidence, not a synonym for physician opinion ([FDA PFDD](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/development-approval-process-drugs/cder-patient-focused-drug-development)).

### Capacity and access are part of the promise

Provider marketing can generate unsafe or wasteful demand if the promoted service has no near-term appointments, accepts only a narrow set of plans, or lacks an accessible referral pathway. For telehealth, licensure can constrain where clinicians may treat patients, with state-specific pathways such as full licenses, compacts, and telehealth registrations ([HHS Telehealth](https://telehealth.hhs.gov/licensure/licensing-across-state-lines)). Marketing operations must ingest capacity, geography, insurance participation, age limits, and clinical exclusion rules before audiences are activated.

## 2. Step 1 — Map stakeholders and design a campaign for each

Use a **stakeholder × decision × evidence × conversion** matrix before creative development.

| Stakeholder | Decision marketing can influence | Most credible value proof | Appropriate conversion | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Patient/consumer | Seek information, ask an HCP, choose provider, enroll, adhere | Understandable absolute benefits/risks, access, cost, lived experience | Symptom guide, appointment, eligibility check, HCP discussion guide | Treating interest as diagnosis; opaque cost or eligibility |
| HCP prescriber | Identify eligible patients, prescribe, monitor | Label, guidelines, peer-reviewed evidence, practical workflow | Rep request, formulary tool, medical-information request, accredited education | Promotional claim drifting beyond label; disguised medical exchange |
| Referring HCP | Choose destination and complete referral | Access, specialist expertise, outcomes, closed-loop communication | Referral order, liaison consult, records transfer | Advertising demand when scheduling and feedback loops are broken |
| Payer | Cover, prefer, manage, contract | Comparative outcomes, budget impact, real-world evidence | Value dossier discussion, pathway review | Using consumer language instead of population economics |
| Employer/benefits buyer | Add a benefit or vendor | Engagement, access, productivity, total cost, implementation evidence | Demo, RFP, pilot | Claiming medical savings from weak observational data |
| Caregiver | Recognize need, navigate, support adherence | Burden reduction, practical instructions, support services | Navigator call, checklist, community support | Assuming authority to receive patient information |

The campaign brief should state one primary stakeholder and one primary behavior. “Awareness among patients and physicians” is not sufficiently specific because the creative, evidence, channels, conversion, and privacy posture differ. A patient campaign may optimize qualified symptom education; an HCP campaign may optimize verified-professional engagement and medically appropriate next steps.

Map influence and payment separately. Ask: Who recognizes the problem? Who diagnoses? Who chooses? Who pays? Who administers? Who can block access? Who supports persistence? In digital health, map the clinical user, economic buyer, IT/security approver, privacy officer, and benefits administrator. In provider marketing, distinguish self-directed patient demand from professional referral volume. For rare disease, add advocacy organizations, specialty laboratories, genetic counselors, centers of excellence, and caregivers.

Finally, translate the matrix into channel guardrails. A payer value story belongs in account-based materials, not consumer social creative. A caregiver checklist can be public, but a navigator’s follow-up must not expose the patient relationship. An HCP audience segment should be based on permissible professional attributes and aggregate behavior, not a consumer’s inferred diagnosis.

## 3. Step 2 — HIPAA-compliant digital marketing

### Start with data flow, not a cookie banner

OCR’s current tracking guidance addresses authenticated and unauthenticated webpages, mobile apps, tracking vendors, BAAs, and disclosures of PHI ([HHS OCR tracking guidance](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/hipaa-online-tracking/index.html)). HIPAA’s marketing rule separately explains when communications are “marketing” and when an authorization may be required ([HHS OCR marketing guidance](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/marketing/index.html)). Consent-management software is not a substitute for HIPAA analysis: ordinary website consent may not satisfy the elements of a valid HIPAA authorization, and a vendor’s standard advertising contract is not a BAA.

Build an event-level inventory:

| Field | Required question |
|---|---|
| Page/app context | Does the URL, screen, title, form, or appointment context reveal health-related intent or a patient relationship? |
| Event | Page view, search, form start, appointment, chat, call, portal login, download, video completion? |
| Data elements | IP address, cookie ID, mobile ad ID, email, phone, URL, diagnosis/service line, provider, timestamp? |
| Recipient | Analytics vendor, ad platform, call tracker, CRM, CDP, agency, data clean room? |
| Purpose | Site operations, measurement, retargeting, suppression, personalization, lead routing? |
| Legal/contractual posture | Covered-entity function, business associate, BAA, authorization, or prohibited disclosure? |
| Retention and onward use | Can the recipient train models, build profiles, or use data for its own advertising? |

HHS’s business-associate guidance explains that a person or entity performing functions involving protected information on behalf of a covered entity may be a business associate, and HHS publishes model BAA provisions ([HHS business associates](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/business-associates/index.html); [HHS model clauses](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/covered-entities/sample-business-associate-agreement-provisions/index.html)). If a vendor will not sign an appropriate BAA, do not solve the problem by hashing an email address and calling it anonymous; hashing is usually pseudonymization, and the surrounding URL, event, and identifier may remain revealing.

### What changed between 2022 and 2024

OCR issued its tracking bulletin in December 2022 and updated it in March 2024. In June 2024, a federal court vacated the portion treating an IP address plus a visit to an unauthenticated public page about a health condition or provider as categorically identifying an individual whose PHI is protected; the American Hospital Association summarized the ruling, and HHS later declined to appeal ([AHA court summary](https://www.aha.org/news/news/2024-06-20-judge-rules-favor-aha-vacating-hhs-online-tracking-bulletin-unlawful-and-beyond-agency-authority); [AHA appeal update](https://www.aha.org/news/headline/2024-08-29-hhs-will-not-appeal-aha-court-victory-online-tracking-case)). The ruling did not create a general safe harbor for Meta Pixel, authenticated pages, appointment flows, or disclosures that actually identify a patient relationship. Marketers should treat the decision as narrowing one theory, not as permission to restore legacy retargeting.

### Safer implementation hierarchy

1. **Minimize collection.** Remove ad tags from portals, symptom checkers, appointment flows, condition pages with strong health intent, and forms unless a documented review supports them.
2. **Separate zones.** Use distinct tag containers and domains for general corporate content versus care-seeking or authenticated experiences. Never let a global tag manager silently propagate into the patient portal.
3. **Use first-party, coarse events.** Prefer aggregated page categories and campaign-level conversions over URL strings, free text, clinician names, diagnosis terms, or patient-level event exports.
4. **Server-side does not mean compliant.** A server-side gateway can strip parameters, rotate identifiers, enforce allowlists, and block onward sharing, but it merely relocates the disclosure unless configured to prevent the vendor from receiving PHI.
5. **Consent mode is a control, not a legal conclusion.** Configure default-denied storage where appropriate and model conversions only from permissible signals; do not pass sensitive event names even in consent-denied pings.
6. **Contract and monitor.** Confirm BAA scope, data ownership, retention, deletion, subprocessors, model-training prohibitions, incident notification, and audit rights. Re-test after every site, SDK, tag-manager, or platform change.

### Enforcement lessons

The FTC’s GoodRx action was its first enforcement under the Health Breach Notification Rule and alleged disclosure of sensitive health information to advertising platforms despite privacy promises ([FTC GoodRx](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/02/ftc-enforcement-action-bar-goodrx-sharing-consumers-sensitive-health-info-advertising)). The BetterHelp order barred sharing health data for advertising and required $7.8 million for partial refunds ([FTC BetterHelp](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/07/ftc-gives-final-approval-order-banning-betterhelp-sharing-sensitive-health-data-advertising)). The FTC’s Cerebral action alleged deceptive and unfair privacy practices and included restrictions on using or disclosing sensitive data for advertising ([FTC Cerebral](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/04/proposed-ftc-order-will-prohibit-telehealth-firm-cerebral-using-or-disclosing-sensitive-data)). Flo Health’s settlement addressed allegations that a fertility app shared sensitive health data with Facebook, Google, and others contrary to promises ([FTC Flo](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2021/06/ftc-finalizes-order-flo-health-fertility-tracking-app-shared-sensitive-health-data-facebook-google)).

Hospital systems also faced costly private pixel litigation: Novant Health agreed to a reported $6.6 million settlement involving pixel allegations, while Advocate Aurora’s litigation reportedly produced a $12.25 million settlement ([HIPAA Journal on Novant](https://www.hipaajournal.com/novant-health-pixel-privacy-breach-settlement/); [Advocate Aurora case summary](https://bluetrusthealth.com/learn/case-law/smith-v-advocate-aurora-pixel-class-action)). These are not OCR settlements and should not be described as such; they demonstrate that HIPAA is only one part of the exposure, alongside state privacy, wiretap, contract, and consumer-protection theories.

## 4. Step 3 — DTC prescription-drug advertising

### Classify before writing

| Ad type | Product named? | Indication/condition discussed? | Core consequence |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Product-claim | Yes | Yes | Benefit claims trigger risk disclosure, fair balance, and adequate support |
| Reminder | Yes | No indication or use | Cannot suggest indication; generally no risk/benefit claims |
| Help-seeking/disease awareness | No specific product | Yes | Avoid linking the disease message so tightly to branded promotion that the pieces become a product claim |
| Institutional | Company only | No product claim | Reputation/capability message; avoid implied product claims |

FDA’s consumer materials and sample-ad library illustrate these categories ([FDA ad overview](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/information-consumers-and-patients-drugs/prescription-drug-advertising); [FDA sample advertisements](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/prescription-drug-advertising/sample-prescription-drug-advertisements)). A correct help-seeking ad describes a condition and encourages consultation without naming a drug ([FDA help-seeking example](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/prescription-drug-advertising/correct-help-seeking-ad)).

### Fair balance is an experience, not a word count

For product-claim advertising, pair each benefit with material risks in comparable prominence and readability. Risk cannot be relegated to a low-contrast footer, flashed briefly, spoken at accelerated speed, or separated from an emotionally dominant efficacy demonstration. FDA’s risk-presentation guidance focuses on both content and format ([FDA risk-information guidance](https://www.fda.gov/media/76269/download)). Build storyboards with side-by-side benefit/risk time, type size, contrast, audio pace, supers, scene intensity, and call-to-action placement.

Broadcast product-claim ads must present a “major statement” of major risks in audio and provide adequate provision for the approved labeling; FDA’s longstanding broadcast guidance describes mechanisms such as toll-free numbers, print availability, healthcare-provider referral, and an internet address ([FDA broadcast guidance](https://www.fda.gov/media/75406/download)). The final rule for DTC television/radio requires the major statement to be presented in a clear, conspicuous, and neutral manner; FDA’s implementation page and final-rule materials should be built into broadcast QA ([FDA CCN rule page](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/direct-consumer-prescription-drug-advertisements-presentation-major-statement-clear-conspicuous-and); [FDA final-rule explainer](https://www.fda.gov/media/184312/download)).

### Retire the “one-click rule” myth

There is no general FDA safe harbor allowing benefit claims in a post, banner, or search ad while placing all risk one click away. FDA’s character-space guidance expects risk information in the same communication when the communication makes benefit claims, with a direct link to fuller risk information ([FDA character-space guidance](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/internetsocial-media-platforms-character-space-limitations-presenting-risk-and-benefit-information)). If a placement cannot accommodate truthful, balanced benefit and risk communication, change the format, use a reminder/help-seeking execution where legally appropriate, or do not run it.

### Creative operating rules

- Create a **label-to-claim matrix**: exact claim, population, endpoint, comparator, limitations, risk pairing, source, channel constraints, expiration trigger.
- Treat visual implication as a claim. Lifestyle imagery, before/after sequencing, speed of relief, caregiver independence, and return-to-work scenes can overstate outcomes even when the headline is technically accurate.
- Separate indication qualification from conversion. Screening copy should not imply that a symptom makes someone an appropriate patient.
- Design important safety information (ISI) as content architecture, not a legal footer: persistent access, readable sections, tested mobile behavior, and no overlays that obscure risk.
- Submit promotional materials in the required format and timing; FDA’s guidance covers electronic and non-electronic submission of promotional labeling and advertising materials ([FDA promotional-material submissions](https://www.fda.gov/media/132975/download)).

FDA’s OPDP publishes warning and untitled letters, which are a practical creative training set ([FDA enforcement-letter index](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/enforcement-activities-fda/warning-letters-and-notice-violation-letters-pharmaceutical-companies); [FDA untitled letters](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/warning-letters-and-notice-violation-letters-pharmaceutical-companies/untitled-letters)). The 2024 Auvi-Q untitled letter is especially useful for reviewing claims embedded in consumer-facing communications ([FDA Auvi-Q letter](https://www.fda.gov/media/180350/download)). Quarterly, code the letters by claim type, omission, evidence issue, format, audience, and remediation; feed the findings into copy templates and reviewer training.

### Make MLR a claim-control system

Medical, legal, and regulatory review should begin with a structured submission rather than a finished layout. The packet should identify audience, objective, channel, claim IDs, label or evidence citations, fair-balance logic, ISI version, mandatory disclosures, linked destinations, targeting method, data collected, and every modular variant. FDA’s OPDP FAQ is a useful baseline for promotional-material responsibilities and submission questions ([FDA OPDP FAQ](https://www.fda.gov/about-fda/center-drug-evaluation-and-research-cder/opdp-frequently-asked-questions-faqs)).

Apply review at three levels. **Claims review** tests support, population, endpoint, comparator, limitations, and risk pairing. **Execution review** tests the complete net impression across words, visuals, audio, sequencing, type, interaction, and device size. **System review** tests the surrounding journey: keyword, audience, influencer instruction, landing page, chatbot, follow-up email, CRM routing, and destination content. A compliant frame can become misleading when a platform crops the ISI, auto-generates copy, places a benefit in the thumbnail, or serves modules in an unapproved order.

Use reusable, versioned modules only when the approved context is preserved. Every asset should inherit the current label and safety block, expire when its evidence or label changes, and be traceable to the exact version that ran. Define expedited lanes for nonsubstantive changes, but never treat shortened copy, new placement, new audience, or localization as automatically administrative; each can change the net impression. Post-launch monitoring should capture rendering defects, comments that introduce claims, adverse-event and product-complaint language, platform-generated variants, and partner deviations.

## 5. Medical devices — clearance/approval and IFU as the marketing perimeter

A 510(k) clearance is a substantial-equivalence determination, while PMA is the premarket approval pathway for higher-risk devices requiring evidence supporting safety and effectiveness; marketers must not collapse “cleared” and “approved” into interchangeable language ([FDA 510(k)](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/premarket-submissions-selecting-and-preparing-correct-submission/premarket-notification-510k); [FDA PMA](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/premarket-submissions-selecting-and-preparing-correct-submission/premarket-approval-pma)).

Build every claim from the authorized intended use, indications, patient population, setting, user, contraindications, warnings, performance data, and IFU. FDA’s device-labeling resources explain that labeling extends beyond the physical label and includes accompanying material ([FDA device labeling](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/overview-device-regulation/device-labeling)). A promotional claim that expands disease severity, anatomical site, patient age, care setting, operator, or clinical outcome can alter the intended-use story even if each noun appears somewhere in technical documentation.

Use a **claim-distance test**:

- **Green:** verbatim or conservative paraphrase of authorized labeling, with matched population and endpoint.
- **Amber:** real-world workflow, economic, convenience, or comparative statement not explicit in labeling; require substantiation and regulatory/legal review.
- **Red:** new diagnostic or therapeutic use, unreviewed patient segment, superior clinical outcome without adequate evidence, or instructions inconsistent with IFU.

Human-factors marketing matters. A video that demonstrates setup differently from the IFU can create misuse risk; an “easy enough for anyone” claim may conflict with trained-user limitations. FDA’s guidance on communications consistent with FDA-required labeling provides a framework for evaluating whether information is consistent, scientifically appropriate, and contextualized, though counsel should apply it to the specific product and communication ([FDA CFL guidance](https://www.fda.gov/media/102575/download)).

Veterinary marketing deserves its own review path: FDA’s Center for Veterinary Medicine states that prescription animal-drug promotion must be truthful, balanced, and accurately communicated ([FDA animal-drug promotion](https://www.fda.gov/animal-veterinary/resources-you/promotion-advertising-prescription-animal-drugs)). Do not import human-DTC templates without adapting species, owner/veterinarian roles, indications, and safety language.

## 6. Step 4 — HCP marketing and medical/commercial boundaries

### Omnichannel with professional utility

HCP omnichannel should orchestrate useful next actions, not repeat a sales message across email, display, rep visits, portals, congresses, and webinars. Segment by specialty, treatment setting, patient mix, access environment, evidence need, and channel preference. A useful sequence might move from congress awareness to a peer-reviewed clinical summary, then to a formulary/access tool, then to a rep or medical-information interaction. Measure verified-HCP reach, content depth, qualified follow-up, new-to-brand starts where permissible, and patient-support activation—not raw email opens.

### Commercial versus medical affairs

Commercial owns promotional strategy within the approved claim set. Medical affairs responds to unsolicited medical questions, supports evidence exchange, engages scientific experts, and generates or communicates data under nonpromotional governance. FDA’s guidance on scientific information to HCPs addresses firm-initiated communications of scientific information on unapproved uses and the need for truthful, nonmisleading, appropriately contextualized presentation ([FDA scientific-information guidance](https://www.fda.gov/media/184871/download)).

Operational safeguards:

- separate objectives, budgets, systems permissions, and performance measures;
- document whether a request was unsolicited and preserve the exact question;
- route off-label questions to trained medical-information staff;
- prohibit sales follow-up that converts a medical exchange into a promotional call;
- label congress spaces and content by function;
- review KOL objectives for scientific need, not prescribing influence.

An MSL should not be measured on prescriptions or market share. Commercial teams may use compliant, approved materials; they should not recruit medical colleagues to deliver off-label messages that sales cannot deliver directly.

### Samples, transfers of value, and peer-to-peer programs

The Prescription Drug Marketing Act governs sample distribution and was designed to protect the integrity of the drug-distribution system; FDA’s Q&A addresses written requests, receipts, inventories, and related controls ([FDA PDMA](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/selected-amendments-fdc-act/prescription-drug-marketing-act-1987); [FDA PDMA Q&A](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/prescription-drug-marketing-act-pdma-requirements-questions-and-answers)). Samples are not event giveaways. Use identity verification, authorized addresses, signed requests/receipts, reconciliation, loss/theft escalation, and separation from incentive compensation.

CMS Open Payments makes applicable-manufacturer payments and other transfers of value to covered recipients publicly available, including categories relevant to consulting, food, travel, education, research, and ownership ([CMS Open Payments overview](https://www.cms.gov/priorities/key-initiatives/open-payments); [CMS Open Payments data](https://www.cms.gov/priorities/key-initiatives/open-payments/data)). The PhRMA Code provides industry standards for interactions with HCPs, including informational presentations, meals, consultants, speakers, and educational support ([PhRMA Code](https://phrma.org/resources/code-on-interactions-with-health-care-professionals)).

For speaker programs and peer-to-peer promotion, require a documented educational need, trained and appropriately qualified speakers, approved content, fair-market-value compensation, attendee eligibility, no repeat-attendance pattern, modest venue/meal, attendance verification, and monitoring. OIG warns that free goods and services can implicate anti-kickback concerns when offered to induce or reward federally reimbursable business ([HHS OIG](https://oig.hhs.gov/fraud/docs/safeharborregulations/provision070197.htm)). Congress sponsorships and advisory boards need the same necessity and documentation discipline; “relationship building” alone is not a defensible purpose.

## 7. Step 5 — Digital health, telehealth, femtech, and mental-health apps

### Certification and platform access

Google restricts healthcare and medicines advertising by product and geography and requires certification for specified categories ([Google healthcare policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/176031?hl=en)). LegitScript offers certification for telemedicine and other healthcare merchants and is recognized in platform-access workflows ([LegitScript telemedicine certification](https://www.legitscript.com/certification/telemedicine/); [LegitScript healthcare certification](https://www.legitscript.com/certification/healthcare-certification/)). Certification is an entry requirement, not a compliance shield: the landing page, prescriber model, pharmacy relationship, claims, state footprint, fulfillment, privacy, and ongoing monitoring still matter.

Google treats health as a sensitive-interest category in personalized advertising, restricting advertiser-curated audiences and certain targeting while allowing only specified alternatives ([Google health personalized-ad policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/16701855?hl=en)). Meta’s health-and-wellness standard restricts categories and claims, including sensitive or potentially unsafe products and practices ([Meta health and wellness](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/restricted-goods-services/health-wellness/)). Platform eligibility changes frequently; maintain a market-by-market matrix of product category, certification, targeting, creative, landing page, age, and measurement constraints.

### Design around sensitive data

Mental health, fertility, sexual health, addiction, weight, and chronic-disease signals deserve heightened controls whether or not the business is a HIPAA covered entity. The GoodRx, BetterHelp, Cerebral, and Flo actions show that consumer-health apps can face FTC exposure for advertising disclosures and broken privacy promises even outside traditional provider workflows ([FTC health privacy cases](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/cases-proceedings?field_consumer_protection_topics=1422&field_mission[29]=29&sort_by=field_date&page=1)).

Do not build retargeting pools from condition-page visits, quiz answers, symptom terms, prescriber selection, medication interest, or appointment behavior. Avoid creative that implies knowledge of the viewer’s condition (“Still depressed?”) or uses shame, body-image pressure, or deterministic diagnosis. Prefer contextual media, broad geography, non-sensitive cohorts, publisher-direct placements, and aggregate experiments.

### Direct-to-patient prescribing

The marketing funnel must encode clinical and geographic eligibility. HHS advises telehealth providers to understand state licensure pathways and behavioral-health-specific requirements ([HHS cross-state licensure](https://telehealth.hhs.gov/licensure/licensing-across-state-lines); [HHS behavioral-health licensure](https://telehealth.hhs.gov/licensure/licensure-for-behavioral-health)). Controlled-substance rules remain a moving target: DEA and HHS extended pandemic-era telemedicine flexibilities through 2025 and later announced additional telemedicine rules, so campaign eligibility should be updated against current federal and state requirements before launch ([DEA 2025 extension](https://www.dea.gov/documents/2024/2024-11/2024-11-15/dea-and-hhs-extend-telemedicine-flexibilities-through-2025); [DEA rule announcement](https://www.dea.gov/press-releases/2025/01/16/dea-announces-three-new-telemedicine-rules-continue-open-access)).

Never promise a prescription before an independent clinical evaluation. Separate marketing qualification from medical intake, disclose material costs and continuity requirements, state that treatment is subject to clinician judgment, and audit whether acquisition incentives pressure prescribing. Cerebral’s case is the caution against optimizing the entire business around rapid conversion while privacy, cancellation, and clinical safeguards lag ([FTC Cerebral case docket](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/cases-proceedings/222-3067-cerebral-inc-kyle-robertson-us-v)).

## 8. Step 6 — Hospital and health-system service-line marketing

### Prioritize where demand can be served

Score service lines on market need, clinical differentiation, payer mix, contribution margin, capacity, access friction, referral leakage, geographic reach, strategic priority, and reputational risk. McKinsey’s “Service Line 2.0” argues for an integrated chronic-disease strategy across the care continuum, with outcomes, quality, and consumer experience—not isolated episodic promotion ([McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/healthcare/our-insights/chronic-disease-excellence-service-line-20-for-health-systems)).

A high-margin line with a twelve-week wait is not automatically a marketing priority. First fix scheduling, call abandonment, referral authorization, clinical triage, and accepted-plan data. Use campaign suppression when capacity thresholds are exceeded and shift spend by location and appointment type.

### Build two growth engines

**Patient acquisition:** condition/service content, paid search, local listings, physician profiles, payer-directory accuracy, call and online scheduling, navigation, and reputation. Optimize from impression to completed appointment and downstream episode—not submitted lead.

**Professional referral development:** physician liaisons, referral analytics, access commitments, specialist-to-referrer communication, CME where appropriate, referral status updates, and service recovery. Liaisons should carry market intelligence and remove friction, not act as gift-bearing sales reps. Track referring-provider activation, referral completion, leakage, time-to-appointment, and closure communication.

### CRM and activation architecture

Use an enterprise master patient index and consent/communication preferences to avoid fragmented outreach. Salesforce Health Cloud supports referral-management resources, while Actium markets patient activation for health systems; Evariant became part of Healthgrades, so architecture discussions should distinguish current supported products from legacy names ([Salesforce referral management](https://developer.salesforce.com/docs/atlas.en-us.health_cloud_object_reference.meta/health_cloud_object_reference/connect_resources_referal_management.htm); [Actium Health](https://www.actiumhealth.com/organizations/health-system/); [Healthgrades acquisition of Evariant](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20200107005267/en/Healthgrades-Acquires-Evariant)).

The stack should connect campaign source, call center, scheduling, referral, encounter, and finance data through privacy-approved identifiers. Avoid dumping clinical detail into a general marketing automation platform. Use role-based access, field minimization, suppression rules for deceased patients and sensitive services, and clear separation between care communications and promotional campaigns.

### Reputation management

Ask for feedback consistently rather than selectively soliciting only satisfied patients. Route service recovery privately. In public responses, do not confirm that the reviewer is a patient, repeat treatment details, or explain the chart. HHS’s HIPAA portal is the governing starting point for covered entities, and every response template should be privacy reviewed ([HHS HIPAA](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/index.html)). A safe response thanks the commenter, states that privacy prevents discussing individual circumstances, and provides a private contact route.

## 9. Step 7 — Local SEO and provider discovery

Google allows public-facing practitioners to have individual Business Profiles when they can be contacted directly at the verified location during stated hours; its representation guidelines also cover departments, names, addresses, and duplicate profiles ([Google Business Profile guidelines](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177?hl=en)). For multi-location systems:

- maintain a canonical facility/location/provider hierarchy and stable IDs;
- standardize names, addresses, phone numbers, categories, hours, appointment URLs, and emergency status;
- create practitioner profiles only where the practitioner is genuinely reachable;
- prevent duplicate profiles during acquisitions, moves, and rebrands;
- publish location-specific accepted insurance, accessibility, parking, languages, telehealth availability, and next-available appointment;
- reconcile website, Google, payer directories, NPI records, and aggregators monthly.

Healthgrades enables physician discovery and insurance-oriented searches, while Zocdoc asks patients to provide plan details to find in-network clinicians and book appointments ([Healthgrades](https://www.healthgrades.com/); [Zocdoc insurance help](https://www.zocdoc.com/patient-help/en/articles/8733187-how-do-i-find-a-doctor-who-takes-my-insurance)). Directory profiles are conversion surfaces, not merely backlinks. Feed credentials, specialties, locations, plans, age limits, languages, appointment type, and availability from an authoritative provider-data source; monitor rejected appointments caused by stale plan data.

For dental, veterinary, physical therapy, behavioral health, and independent practices, local intent is often the highest-value demand. Build pages around actual services and locations rather than doorway pages for every suburb. Include clinician credentials, transparent access, accepted plans/payment options, and an accurate booking path. Track calls and bookings without exposing query terms, conditions, or patient identifiers to advertising recipients.

## 10. Step 8 — Medical content under YMYL and E-E-A-T

Google’s people-first guidance asks creators to demonstrate first-hand expertise, clear sourcing, and trustworthiness; for topics affecting health or safety, trust expectations are especially high ([Google helpful-content guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content); [Google Search Quality overview](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/hsw-sqrg.pdf)). E-E-A-T is not a score to “add” with an author box. It is an operating system for editorial accountability.

A defensible workflow includes:

1. **Intent and harm assessment:** what could a reader do incorrectly because of this page?
2. **Evidence brief:** guidelines, labeling, systematic reviews, primary studies, and currency date.
3. **Qualified author:** match expertise to the question; a general copywriter should not independently interpret treatment evidence.
4. **Medical review:** named reviewer, credentials, review date, conflicts, and explicit check of benefit, risk, alternatives, and red flags.
5. **Plain-language QA:** distinguish education from diagnosis; use absolute numbers where possible; define uncertainty.
6. **Update governance:** triggers for guideline, safety, label, product, or standard-of-care changes.
7. **Correction mechanism:** visible contact and rapid clinical escalation.

Mayo Clinic states that its health information is produced through an evidence-based process and identifies medical editors ([Mayo Clinic health-information policy](https://www.mayoclinic.org/about-this-site/health-information-policy); [Mayo medical editors](https://www.mayoclinic.org/about-this-site/meet-our-medical-editors)). Cleveland Clinic’s editorial policy describes medical review, sourcing, updates, and separation of editorial content from advertising ([Cleveland Clinic editorial policy](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/about/website/editorial-policy)). The strategic lesson is process visibility: credentials, sources, dates, and editorial independence make content more trustworthy to both users and discovery systems.

Avoid prevalence inflation, symptom-list bait, unsupported causality, false urgency, and headlines that outrun the evidence. AI can assist outlining and readability, but clinical assertions need source verification and accountable review. For user-generated content, moderate misinformation, crisis language, and product claims; do not let testimonials become unreviewed efficacy claims.

## 11. Step 9 — Medicare and Medicaid marketing

### Medicare Advantage and Part D

CMS distinguishes communications from marketing and publishes Medicare Communications and Marketing Guidelines, model materials, and plan-year instructions ([CMS Medicare marketing guidelines](https://www.cms.gov/medicare/health-drug-plans/managed-care-marketing/medicare-guidelines); [CMS models and standardized materials](https://www.cms.gov/medicare/health-drug-plans/managed-care-marketing/models-standard-documents-educational-materials)). Marketing teams need current-year controls for material review/submission, required disclaimers, benefit presentation, provider/pharmacy claims, Star Ratings references, contact rules, events, websites, call centers, agents, and third-party marketing organizations (TPMOs).

The annual Medicare Open Enrollment Period runs October 15 through December 7 for coverage effective January 1 ([Medicare.gov](https://www.medicare.gov/health-drug-plans/open-enrollment)). The Medicare Advantage Open Enrollment Period runs January 1 through March 31 for people already enrolled in Medicare Advantage, with narrower switching rights ([Medicare.gov enrollment periods](https://www.medicare.gov/basics/get-started-with-medicare/get-more-coverage/joining-a-plan)). Do not treat OEP as a second AEP prospecting season; configure audience eligibility and agent scripts to the allowed election context.

TPMO and lead-generation controls became a major focus beginning in 2023. CMS’s agent/broker FAQ addresses recording, disclaimers, and oversight questions for marketing activity ([CMS agent/broker FAQ](https://www.cms.gov/files/document/agent-broker-marketing-faqs-10-19-2022.pdf)). The CY 2025 final rule strengthened beneficiary protections and changed agent/broker compensation and contracting rules, reinforcing plan accountability for downstream actors ([CMS CY 2025 final rule](https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/fact-sheets/contract-year-2025-medicare-advantage-part-d-final-rule-cms-4205-f)).

Operationally:

- inventory every agency, lead seller, call center, field marketing organization, and sub-agent;
- approve creative and landing-page variants before use;
- preserve consent, scope-of-appointment, call recordings, version history, and lead provenance;
- test agents with mystery shopping and call analytics;
- prohibit misleading “Medicare” branding, unsupported savings, unavailable benefits, and bait-and-switch lead forms;
- reconcile provider, drug, formulary, pharmacy, and supplemental-benefit claims against plan data;
- monitor complaints, rapid disenrollment, canceled enrollments, and vulnerable-population patterns by source.

### Medicaid

Medicaid marketing rules vary by state and managed-care contract. Build a state-by-state matrix covering outreach, member materials, enrollment assistance, gifts, language/accessibility, subcontractor approval, and use of state marks. Federal managed-care rules require enrollment and informational materials to be understandable and establish information requirements for enrollees and potential enrollees ([Medicaid managed-care regulations](https://www.medicaid.gov/Medicaid/downloads/managed-care-regulations-42-cfr-part-438.pdf)); the specific state contract and agency guidance govern execution. Never import an MA playbook unchanged merely because the same payer and agency are involved.

## 12. Step 10 — Supplements and OTC marketing

### Structure/function versus disease claims

Under DSHEA, dietary supplements may make certain structure/function claims—for example, describing support of normal body structure or function—if they are truthful, not misleading, substantiated, notified to FDA as required, and accompanied by the statutory disclaimer. They may not claim to diagnose, mitigate, treat, cure, or prevent disease without entering drug territory ([FDA structure/function claims](https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-food-labeling-and-critical-foods/structurefunction-claims); [FDA notification guidance](https://www.fda.gov/food/information-industry-dietary-supplements/notifications-structurefunction-and-related-claims-dietary-supplement-labeling)).

The line depends on net impression, not a keyword swap. “Supports immune health” can become an implied disease claim when paired with pathogen imagery, an outbreak headline, a white coat, or testimonials about avoiding infection. Product names, hashtags, search terms, influencer scripts, FAQs, metadata, and retailer content all contribute to the message.

### FTC substantiation

The FTC’s Health Products Compliance Guidance says health-related claims must be truthful, not misleading, and supported by competent and reliable scientific evidence; as a general matter, randomized, controlled human clinical testing is the most reliable form of evidence for health benefits ([FTC Health Products Compliance Guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance)). Disclaimers cannot cure a contradictory headline or an overall impression of proven treatment. Match the evidence to the exact product, dose, population, endpoint, duration, and claim.

Create a substantiation dossier with the claimed benefit, express and implied messages, study design, product equivalence, effect size, limitations, contrary evidence, safety, and qualified language. Review testimonials as claims: “my arthritis disappeared” communicates treatment even if the brand only supplied the quote. The FTC’s Prevagen litigation illustrates the enforcement risk around memory claims and scientific support ([FTC Prevagen statement](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/12/statement-ftcs-win-lawsuit-against-makers-dietary-supplement-prevagen)). FDA’s health-fraud warning-letter collection supplies current examples of disease claims and unsafe positioning ([FDA 2024 health-fraud letters](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/health-fraud-scams/2024-warning-letters-health-fraud)).

### OTC and retail platforms

OTC drug claims should align with the applicable monograph or approved labeling, including indications, directions, age limits, warnings, and dosage. “Clinically proven” requires proof for the advertised proposition, not merely a compliant active ingredient.

Amazon Ads requires claims to be supported and maintains healthcare-product advertising restrictions; marketplace listing permission and paid-ad approval are separate reviews ([Amazon claims policy](https://advertising.amazon.com/help/G6UU66YLSCUYDULQ); [Amazon healthcare ads policy](https://advertising.amazon.com/help/GPSSMTEAA6AEWY3A)). Maintain a retailer-specific claim library, monitor reseller edits, and audit product detail pages, A+ content, sponsored ads, reviews used in creative, and search keywords.

## 13. Step 11 — Patient advocacy, disease awareness, and rare disease

Unbranded disease-awareness campaigns are most useful when diagnosis is delayed, symptoms are normalized, or referral pathways are unclear. FDA research notes that viewers can conflate disease-awareness and prescription-drug communications, so separation must be substantive rather than cosmetic ([FDA disease-awareness research](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/spotlight-cder-science/disease-awareness-and-prescription-drug-communications-television-evidence-conflation-and-misleading)). Avoid product colors, taglines, URLs, imagery, timing, or retargeting sequences that effectively identify one treatment while omitting its risks.

A high-integrity campaign should:

- define a clinically meaningful audience without implying diagnosis;
- explain symptoms, red flags, diagnostic pathway, and appropriate specialists;
- acknowledge differential diagnoses and uncertainty;
- provide independent resources and crisis support where relevant;
- separate advocacy grants, patient education, research recruitment, and product promotion;
- disclose sponsorship and data practices plainly.

Rare-disease marketing requires special restraint because communities are small, identifiable, and highly networked; evidence is often limited, diagnosis is delayed, and caregivers carry large burdens. Patient organizations can surface language, journey barriers, and endpoints, but engagement should not become a covert endorsement channel. NORD’s Corporate Council Code of Conduct emphasizes ethical collaboration and organizational independence ([NORD Code of Conduct](https://rarediseases.org/corporate-council/code-of-conduct/)). FDA’s rare-disease development guidance recognizes the distinct challenges of small, heterogeneous populations ([FDA rare-disease guidance](https://www.fda.gov/media/119757/download)).

Compensate patient advisers at fair market value for defined work; disclose company support; obtain permission for stories; minimize identifiable detail; and offer withdrawal procedures. Do not make participation in an advocacy community a lead-generation event. In ultra-rare conditions, even age, geography, mutation, and treatment history may identify someone, so aggregate reporting requires more than deleting a name.

## 14. Step 12 — Measurement in healthcare

### Match metrics to the healthcare decision

| Business | Leading indicators | Outcome metrics | Design caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pharma DTC | Qualified reach, disease education, HCP discussion intent | New-to-brand Rx, total Rx, starts, adherence/persistence, incremental patients | Control for formulary, seasonality, rep activity, samples, and diagnosis trends |
| HCP promotion | Verified reach, engagement with evidence, rep/medical requests | Prescribing among eligible HCPs, breadth/depth, formulary pull-through | Avoid off-label inference and tiny-cell re-identification |
| Provider | Calls, bookings, referral orders, appointment availability | Completed visits, procedures, episodes, contribution margin, leakage | Capacity and insurance mismatch can make media look ineffective |
| Payer/MA | Qualified calls, compliant appointments, plan comparisons | Valid enrollments, retention, complaint rate, rapid disenrollment | Optimize to member fit, not gross enrollment |
| Digital health | Eligibility, activation, first clinical encounter | Engagement, clinical outcome, retention, total cost where validated | App events may reveal sensitive health information |
| Supplement/OTC | Detail-page engagement, retailer conversion | Incremental sales, repeat, lift tests | Do not optimize into unsupported claim variants |

IQVIA describes data assets that include prescription, sales, claims, and related healthcare information used for market analysis ([IQVIA available data](https://www.iqvia.com/insights/the-iqvia-institute/available-iqvia-data)). Its DTC case materials illustrate net-impact analysis linking media exposure to prescription outcomes ([IQVIA DTC measurement](https://www.iqvia.com/-/media/iqvia/pdfs/us/case-study/2023/net-rx-impact-analysis-proves-the-value-of-dtc-point-of-care-media.pdf)). Symphony Health offers patient-level longitudinal data products, while Komodo markets patient-journey and campaign-measurement capabilities ([Symphony PatientSource](https://s3.us-east-1.amazonaws.com/prahs-symphony-health/resources/Sales-Sheet_SH_PatientSource_08272021.pdf); [Komodo measurement](https://www.komodohealth.com/press/komodo-health-introduces-new-marketing-measurement-offering-to-enhance-multichannel-campaign-performance-for-life-sciences-brands/)). Vendor claims should be validated against permitted use, coverage, lag, projection methodology, tokenization, match bias, and minimum-cell controls.

### A practical hierarchy of evidence

1. **Randomized geo, provider, or audience holdout** where operationally and ethically feasible.
2. **Matched-control or difference-in-differences** using pre-period trends and access variables.
3. **Interrupted time series** for launches or policy changes with sufficient history.
4. **Marketing-mix modeling (MMM)** for aggregated channel contribution when user-level tracking is restricted.
5. **Attribution** for directional optimization, not causal truth.

For Rx lift, define exposed and control populations before reading outcomes, require washout and follow-up windows, control for baseline prescribing and access, report confidence intervals, and distinguish initiation from refills. Do not claim campaign causality from a post-exposure prescription correlation.

MMM is increasingly useful because it operates on aggregate time-series inputs and can incorporate TV, search, HCP promotion, samples, field force, formulary, seasonality, epidemiology, and competitor activity. IQVIA describes pharmaceutical marketing-mix approaches as a way to estimate channel contribution and optimize allocation ([IQVIA MMM](https://www.iqvia.com/locations/emea/blogs/2024/11/introduction-of-marketing-mix-in-the-pharmaceutical-industry)). Its limitations are important: sparse variation, correlated channels, changing access, and long lag structures can create unstable estimates. Use experiments to calibrate the model.

Measurement governance should include a purpose register, minimum necessary fields, approved linkage vendors, BAA/data-use terms where applicable, cohort thresholds, suppression, retention, and output review. A “clean room” is not inherently safe; evaluate inputs, joins, outputs, and each party’s independent use rights.

## 15. Common anti-patterns and enforcement lessons

| Anti-pattern | Why it fails | Better control | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Benefit headline, risk behind a click | Risk is not presented with the benefit; “one click” is not a safe harbor | Same-communication risk, appropriate format, full-risk link | [FDA character-space guidance](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/internetsocial-media-platforms-character-space-limitations-presenting-risk-and-benefit-information) |
| “FDA approved” for a 510(k)-cleared device | Misstates the regulatory pathway and may imply a stronger determination | Use exact clearance/approval language and authorized indication | [FDA device approvals and clearances](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/products-and-medical-procedures/device-approvals-and-clearances) |
| Pixel on appointment confirmation page | Can disclose patient relationship, service, identifiers, and conversion to an ad vendor | Remove; use privacy-reviewed first-party measurement | [HHS OCR tracking guidance](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/hipaa-online-tracking/index.html) |
| Hashing email before upload | Hashing may not remove identifiability or sensitive context | Avoid upload or use a contractually controlled, minimum-necessary design | [FTC GoodRx](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/02/ftc-enforcement-action-bar-goodrx-sharing-consumers-sensitive-health-info-advertising) |
| Medical affairs as off-label sales support | Converts scientific exchange into promotion and undermines independence | Separate objectives, systems, training, and follow-up | [FDA scientific-information guidance](https://www.fda.gov/media/184871/download) |
| Repeated dinner programs for the same HCPs | Educational rationale weakens; inducement and transfer-of-value risk rises | Need-based programs, FMV, attendance analytics, monitoring | [PhRMA Code](https://phrma.org/resources/code-on-interactions-with-health-care-professionals) |
| Lead-form optimization for MA | Can reward misleading volume and obscure downstream brokers | Source-level consent, recordings, complaint and rapid-disenrollment metrics | [CMS CY 2025 final rule](https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/fact-sheets/contract-year-2025-medicare-advantage-part-d-final-rule-cms-4205-f) |
| Supplement testimonial used verbatim | Consumer experience becomes an implied treatment claim | Translate only into substantiated, qualified claims—or do not use | [FTC health guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance) |
| Replying to a negative review with chart facts | Publicly confirms care and discloses health information | Neutral acknowledgement and private service-recovery route | [HHS HIPAA](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/index.html) |
| Buying more demand into a closed schedule | Wastes spend and worsens experience | Capacity feed, location routing, campaign throttles | [Deloitte patient engagement](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/industry/health-care/patient-consumerism-health-care-engagement.html) |
| Unbranded campaign visually tethered to one brand | Audience may conflate disease education with product promotion | Genuine separation of creative, data, cadence, and conversion | [FDA disease-awareness research](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/spotlight-cder-science/disease-awareness-and-prescription-drug-communications-television-evidence-conflation-and-misleading) |

OPDP enforcement should be reviewed as pattern data, not anecdotes. Common themes include omitted/minimized risk, overstated efficacy, unsupported superiority, broadening the indicated population, and misleading presentation. The OPDP office explains its mandate to protect public health by helping ensure prescription-drug promotion is truthful, balanced, and accurately communicated ([FDA OPDP](https://www.fda.gov/about-fda/cder-offices-and-divisions/office-prescription-drug-promotion-opdp)).

For consumer health, the FTC cases show a second pattern: the marketing stack itself becomes the violation when sensitive data is disclosed contrary to promises. BetterHelp, GoodRx, Cerebral, and Flo span therapy, prescriptions, telehealth, and fertility, indicating that “we are not a HIPAA covered entity” is not a marketing-risk strategy ([FTC BetterHelp](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/cases-proceedings/2023169-betterhelp-inc-matter); [FTC GoodRx](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/02/ftc-enforcement-action-bar-goodrx-sharing-consumers-sensitive-health-info-advertising); [FTC Flo](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/cases-proceedings/192-3133-flo-health-inc)).

## 16. A 90-day intake for a healthcare marketing client

### Days 0–15: common diagnostic

**Business and stakeholder map**
- Define products/services, indications or service lines, geographies, payers, sites, prescribers/referrers, caregivers, and economic buyers.
- Identify the one business behavior each active campaign is meant to change.
- Map access barriers: formulary, prior authorization, licensure, capacity, insurance participation, referral prerequisites, shipping, age, and clinical eligibility.

**Claims and content inventory**
- Export all live ads, landing pages, emails, sales materials, social posts, influencer assets, directory profiles, call scripts, and partner content.
- Attach each claim to label/IFU, clinical evidence, guideline, substantiation, or operational source.
- Flag expired evidence, missing risk, unsupported comparison, implied diagnosis, and inconsistent cost/access language.

**Data and vendor map**
- Scan websites/apps and manually test portals, symptom tools, forms, calls, chat, scheduling, and thank-you pages.
- Record every tag, SDK, server-side endpoint, custom audience, CRM sync, clean room, data broker, and measurement vendor.
- Collect BAAs, data-use agreements, platform terms, consent language, retention, deletion, and subprocessors.

**Baseline scorecard**
- Spend, reach, qualified conversions, downstream outcomes, capacity, complaints, opt-outs, approval cycle time, and privacy incidents.
- Establish holdouts or pre-periods before changing the media mix.

### Days 16–45: three specialized tracks

#### Track A — Pharma/biotech

1. Build the label-to-claim matrix by audience and channel.
2. Separate commercial, medical, disease-awareness, advocacy, and patient-support workstreams.
3. Audit DTC storyboards for fair balance, ISI, major statement, adequate provision, visual implication, and mobile rendering.
4. Review HCP segmentation, sample controls, Open Payments capture, speaker programs, congress activity, KOL contracts, and MSL handoffs.
5. Read the last 24 months of relevant OPDP letters and code risk patterns from FDA’s current indexes ([FDA warning/untitled letters](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/enforcement-activities-fda/warning-letters-and-notice-violation-letters-pharmaceutical-companies)).
6. Design Rx-lift measurement with pre-specified cohorts, access controls, lag windows, privacy thresholds, and an experimental calibration.

**Day-45 deliverables:** approved claim library, channel guardrails, revised DTC/HCP journeys, speaker/sample remediation plan, and measurement protocol.

#### Track B — Provider/health system

1. Rank service lines using demand, margin, differentiation, capacity, payer fit, leakage, and strategic importance.
2. Mystery-shop call center, scheduling, referrals, insurance verification, and mobile conversion.
3. Reconcile facility and provider data across website, Google Business Profile, payers, Healthgrades, Zocdoc, and internal directories.
4. Audit reputation responses for privacy and service-recovery quality.
5. Redesign physician-liaison territories and objectives around referral friction and closed-loop communication.
6. Remove or redesign risky trackers and connect campaign source to completed encounters through a privacy-approved architecture.

**Day-45 deliverables:** service-line portfolio, access-friction backlog, directory master, liaison plan, tracking remediation, and appointment/encounter dashboard.

#### Track C — Digital health/telehealth

1. Determine HIPAA covered-entity/business-associate status, FTC Health Breach Notification Rule exposure, and sensitive-data promises.
2. Map prescriber, pharmacy, licensure, controlled-substance, age, state, and clinical eligibility rules into the funnel.
3. Document Google/Meta eligibility and LegitScript certification by geography and category.
4. Remove sensitive retargeting and prohibit condition-bearing events from ad-platform payloads.
5. Audit cancellation, continuity, pricing, emergency/crisis routing, clinician independence, and ad-to-intake handoff.
6. Establish app-event governance and an aggregate experimentation plan.

**Day-45 deliverables:** market eligibility matrix, privacy-safe event taxonomy, platform certification plan, compliant funnel, and clinical-escalation map.

### Days 46–75: build and pilot

- Remediate highest-risk assets first: active misleading claims, unsafe pixels, broken access promises, and unmonitored downstream partners.
- Launch one controlled pilot per priority stakeholder with a clear holdout or baseline.
- Train marketers, agencies, call centers, liaisons, reps, MSLs, and community managers on role-specific scenarios.
- Configure approval metadata: audience, claim IDs, risk block, evidence date, owner, market, expiration, and permitted channels.
- Implement daily capacity and eligibility feeds where patient acquisition is involved.
- Stand up complaint, adverse-event/product-complaint, privacy, crisis, and misinformation escalation paths.

### Days 76–90: prove, govern, scale

- Read results at the downstream outcome level and separate marketing failure from access, coverage, scheduling, or clinical-fit failure.
- Audit a sample of impressions, landing sessions, calls, referrals, prescriptions/enrollments, and vendor outputs end to end.
- Establish monthly claims/content review, quarterly tag/SDK review, semiannual vendor review, and annual role-based training.
- Create a stop/go rubric: pause when risk content fails, capacity breaches threshold, eligibility logic breaks, complaint rate spikes, or a vendor changes data use.
- Present a 12-month roadmap with growth upside, remediation cost, evidence gaps, testing calendar, and accountable owners.

### Minimum executive dashboard after 90 days

| Dimension | Pharma | Provider | Digital health |
|---|---|---|---|
| Growth | Incremental starts/Rx, access-adjusted ROI | Completed visits/procedures, referral growth, contribution margin | Eligible activation, completed consults, retained users |
| Experience | Patient-support activation, persistence | Time-to-appointment, call abandonment, referral closure | Time-to-care, cancellation, continuity, crisis routing |
| Compliance | Claim exceptions, OPDP themes, sample/speaker anomalies | Privacy events, directory error, review-response exceptions | Sensitive-event leakage, certification status, prescribing exceptions |
| Evidence | Claim freshness, study fit | Content review currency, outcomes proof | Clinical/economic claim substantiation |
| Equity/access | Reach and support by eligible population | Access by geography, language, payer | Eligibility and outcomes by market without unsafe microtargeting |

## Closing principle

The best healthcare marketing system does not ask compliance to rescue finished creative. It makes the clinical evidence, authorized claim perimeter, patient privacy, stakeholder role, access reality, and measurement design visible before market media is purchased. That discipline improves both safety and performance: fewer misleading messages, fewer unusable leads, more qualified conversations, cleaner causal learning, and a stronger basis for durable trust.
