# Marketing Playbook for a Novel-Category Startup

## Executive summary

A category-creating startup must market two things at once: **the problem and a new way of solving it**. Conventional demand capture assumes buyers already recognize a solution class and are comparing vendors. A novel-category company instead faces an earlier cognitive task: make a costly or frustrating status quo visible, give it memorable language, explain why existing alternatives are structurally inadequate, and only then make its product the credible answer. Category creation is therefore not the same as being first to market; category creators shape how customers understand the problem and the criteria by which solutions should be judged ([Harvard Business Review](https://hbr.org/2019/11/the-difference-between-a-first-mover-and-a-category-creator)).

The practical sequence is:

1. discover a high-intensity problem through evidence about actual behavior;
2. position the product relative to what customers would otherwise do;
3. define and name the category only after the positioning evidence is clear;
4. concentrate on a beachhead segment where the problem is urgent;
5. encode the category narrative in a lean, coherent brand system;
6. test acquisition channels systematically and concentrate resources;
7. educate the market with distinctive, evidence-rich content;
8. turn founders, customers, and communities into credible messengers;
9. use earned media and analysts to validate the problem and language;
10. measure movement from problem recognition to value realization, not exposure alone.

The ordering matters. Naming a category before understanding the customer tends to produce jargon. Scaling channels before establishing message-market fit amplifies confusion. Broadening before winning a beachhead dilutes proof. The playbook below treats category design as a disciplined marketing hypothesis—not a slogan—and uses customer language, channel tests, qualitative feedback, and behavioral metrics to refine it.

## The playbook at a glance

| Step | Core question | Primary output | Early proof |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Customer discovery | Is the problem real, frequent, and costly? | Evidence repository; problem/JTBD map | Repeated past behavior, workarounds, spending, urgency |
| 2. Positioning and messaging | Why this, for whom, instead of what? | Positioning canvas; message hierarchy | Target buyers accurately retell the value proposition |
| 3. Define the category | What new mental folder should exist? | Category name; POV; problem manifesto | Prospects use the language unprompted |
| 4. Pick a beachhead | Where can the company become the obvious choice first? | ICP and beachhead scorecard | High response, conversion, and referenceability in one segment |
| 5. Brand foundations | How will every touchpoint teach the same idea? | Lean identity and narrative system | Recognition and message consistency |
| 6. Select channels | Which route repeatedly reaches the beachhead? | Bullseye test backlog and 1–2 priorities | One channel shows repeatable economics or compounding reach |
| 7. Content and thought leadership | How will the market learn the problem and category? | Editorial architecture and flagship assets | Qualified engagement, assisted conversion, category-language adoption |
| 8. Community and evangelism | Who besides the company will carry the idea? | Founder voice, advocate loop, community ritual | Referrals, member contributions, peer-to-peer advocacy |
| 9. PR and analyst relations | What makes the new category credible and newsworthy? | Evidence-led story angles; briefing kit | Accurate third-party repetition of the problem/category thesis |
| 10. Measurement | Is awareness becoming understanding, intent, and realized value? | Category-learning funnel; North Star and guardrails | Cohort improvement from recognition through activation/retention |

## 1. The special problem: marketing a category-creating startup

### Traditional playbooks only partly apply

Search ads, comparison pages, and feature-based competitive selling work best when a recognized category already supplies buyer language and purchase criteria. A category creator cannot rely on that inherited demand. Its audience may be **problem-aware but not solution-aware**: people feel a pain or use a workaround, but do not know that a dedicated solution class exists. Marketing must therefore progress through a learning sequence: name the problem, challenge the default, introduce the category, demonstrate a new mechanism, and supply proof.

This does **not** justify declaring an empty market or avoiding competition. April Dunford’s positioning method begins with “competitive alternatives”—what customers would do if the product did not exist—because the relevant competitor may be a spreadsheet, an agency, a manual process, a general-purpose tool, or doing nothing ([April Dunford](https://www.aprildunford.com/post/an-introduction-to-positioning)). A new category is credible only when it organizes a real struggle better than customers’ current mental model.

Category creation also differs from first-mover advantage. HBR cautions that observers often conflate the two and describes category creation as broader than launching a breakthrough product ([Harvard Business Review](https://hbr.org/2019/11/the-difference-between-a-first-mover-and-a-category-creator)). The implication for marketing is that “we are first” is weak positioning; the stronger claim is “the world has changed, the old approach now fails for a specific reason, and this new category is the logical response.”

### The category-design job

The category-design firm behind *Play Bigger* frames its blueprint as **define the problem, frame the category, mobilize the company, and own the market** ([Play Bigger](https://www.playbigger.com/)). For an early-stage marketer, this translates into four linked artifacts:

- **Problem frame:** a precise statement of the changed condition, affected audience, cost of inaction, and inadequacy of the status quo.
- **Category point of view:** a falsifiable argument about why a new approach is needed now.
- **Category name:** a short, pronounceable label that creates a useful mental folder without demanding a paragraph of explanation.
- **Proof system:** customer evidence, demonstrations, benchmark data, expert validation, and repeatable use cases that make the frame believable.

Category creation has unusually high upside but the evidence is not a guarantee. HBR reports the frequently cited finding that category creators can capture a disproportionate share of category value, while also warning that category creation and first-mover status are not interchangeable ([Harvard Business Review](https://hbr.org/2013/03/why-it-pays-to-be-a-category-creator); [Harvard Business Review](https://hbr.org/2019/11/the-difference-between-a-first-mover-and-a-category-creator)). Treat category leadership as an earned outcome of market adoption, not a title the startup can award itself.

### Three simultaneous marketing risks

1. **Comprehension risk:** buyers do not understand what the product is or where to place it.
2. **priority risk:** buyers understand it but do not regard the problem as urgent enough to change behavior.
3. **credibility risk:** buyers accept the need but doubt that a small company or unfamiliar mechanism can deliver.

The playbook should diagnose these separately. More reach will not fix poor comprehension; sharper copy will not fix low priority; and a louder category claim will not substitute for proof.

## 2. Step 1 — Deep customer discovery and problem validation

### Objective

Discover the language, context, trigger, current alternative, consequences, and desired progress surrounding a real problem. Jobs-to-be-Done theory treats choices as attempts to make progress in a particular circumstance, rather than as expressions of demographic traits alone ([Christensen Institute](https://www.christenseninstitute.org/theory/jobs-to-be-done/)). This is especially useful when no product category yet exists: customers may not be able to request the solution, but they can reconstruct the struggle, workaround, and moment that caused them to seek change.

### Interview for evidence, not approval

*The Mom Test* is explicitly about learning from customer conversations even when people are inclined to be polite or encouraging ([The Mom Test](https://www.momtestbook.com/)). Use that principle by asking about **specific past behavior**, not opinions about a pitch:

- “Walk through the last time this happened.”
- “What triggered you to act?”
- “What did you try first, and what happened?”
- “Who else was involved?”
- “What did it cost in time, money, risk, anxiety, or missed opportunity?”
- “What are you using now?”
- “What made the problem important—or easy to postpone?”
- “Where did you look for help, and what words did you search?”

Avoid “Would you use this?”, “Do you like the idea?”, and leading category language. Those questions measure courtesy and imagination. Behavioral detail—an improvised spreadsheet, repeated manual work, a paid consultant, abandoned attempts, or a deadline—is stronger evidence of a marketing opportunity.

### Build a marketing evidence repository

For every interview, record:

- verbatim phrases describing the problem and desired outcome;
- triggering event and urgency;
- current alternative and switching friction;
- measurable and emotional consequences;
- information sources, communities, creators, search terms, and trusted experts;
- buying participants for B2B, or household/social influencers for B2C;
- objections and proof required.

Cluster interviews by **struggle and circumstance**, then compare segment patterns. JTBD research emphasizes the circumstances in which customers “hire” a solution, so the most useful early segmentation often combines role or identity with trigger and problem intensity ([Christensen Institute](https://www.christenseninstitute.org/theory/jobs-to-be-done/); [Harvard Business Review](https://hbr.org/podcast/2020/01/revisiting-jobs-to-be-done-with-clayton-christensen)).

### Validation standard

Do not set a universal interview count as a false threshold. Continue until the team can predict the next interview’s main problem pattern and can identify a segment with repeated, consequential behavior. Strong signals include:

- the same problem appears without prompting;
- people have already built or paid for workarounds;
- a trigger creates a bounded window for action;
- the audience can name a cost of remaining with the status quo;
- the same channels and trusted voices recur;
- prospects volunteer introductions, request follow-up, join a waitlist, or take another costly action.

**B2B difference:** map the user, champion, economic buyer, approver, and “hidden” influencers; Edelman and LinkedIn report that B2B decisions involve broader buying groups and that internal misalignment stalls many deals ([Edelman–LinkedIn B2B Thought Leadership Impact Report](https://www.edelman.com/expertise/Business-Marketing/2025-b2b-thought-leadership-report)). **B2C difference:** emphasize context, habit, identity, social proof, and the trigger that moves a person from passive discomfort to action; electronic word-of-mouth research shows that peer information affects attitudes and behavior through mechanisms including credibility and usefulness ([peer-reviewed literature review in PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5524892/)).

### Exit criterion

Advance when the startup can write a one-page problem brief using customer language, identify the current alternatives, describe the trigger and cost of inaction, and name at least one reachable segment where the problem is intense. If the brief depends on product jargon, discovery is incomplete.

## 3. Step 2 — Positioning and messaging

### Position before writing copy

Positioning establishes the context that makes the product’s value obvious. Dunford’s framework connects five components: competitive alternatives, differentiated capabilities, the value those capabilities enable, the customers who care most, and the market category that makes the value understandable ([April Dunford](https://www.aprildunford.com/post/a-quickstart-guide-to-positioning)). Work through them in that order:

1. **Competitive alternatives:** what would the customer do if the product did not exist?
2. **Unique attributes:** what can this approach do that alternatives cannot?
3. **Value:** what outcome does each differentiated attribute enable?
4. **Best-fit customers:** who cares intensely about that value and why?
5. **Market context:** what frame helps those customers understand the value fastest?

This order prevents a common category-creator error: inventing a new category name before proving differentiated value.

### Construct the message architecture

Create a hierarchy that can be tested independently:

- **Audience and trigger:** “For [specific audience] when [recognizable circumstance] …”
- **Problem:** “The old way causes [consequence] because [structural reason].”
- **Category:** “[Category] is a new approach that [plain-language definition].”
- **Value promise:** “It helps [audience] achieve [outcome] without [major trade-off].”
- **Mechanism:** “Unlike [alternative], it works by [distinctive mechanism].”
- **Proof:** “Evidence includes [customer result, demonstration, benchmark, expert, or adoption behavior].”
- **Action:** one low-friction next step appropriate to awareness level.

Messaging should meet audiences at different states. For **problem-unaware** prospects, lead with a familiar symptom or changed environment. For **problem-aware** prospects, quantify the cost and expose why the workaround fails. For **solution-aware** prospects, define the category and mechanism. For **category-aware** prospects, use differentiation, proof, and risk reversal.

### Test message-market fit

Use message tests before expensive distribution:

- **five-second comprehension:** after brief exposure, can a target customer say who it is for, what problem it addresses, and what changes?
- **retell test:** can the customer explain it accurately to a colleague or friend without the founder’s language collapsing into generic terms?
- **contrast test:** can the customer identify the alternative and meaningful difference?
- **resonance test:** does the message prompt a relevant story, question, or next action?
- **behavior test:** compare qualified response, demo/waitlist conversion, or activation by message—not clicks alone.

The best copy is usually mined from discovery, but customer wording is not automatically strategy. Marketing must combine recognizable language with a distinctive interpretation of the problem. Dunford’s emphasis on alternatives, differentiated capabilities, and best-fit customers supplies the strategic constraint ([April Dunford](https://www.aprildunford.com/post/an-introduction-to-positioning)).

### Exit criterion

Advance when target customers understand the problem and value without a founder explanation, can distinguish the product from the status quo, and show materially stronger behavior for one message frame than for plausible alternatives.

## 4. Step 3 — Define and name the category

### Write the category point of view

A defensible category POV answers six questions:

1. **What changed?** A technology, behavior, regulation, cost curve, cultural norm, or market structure.
2. **Who is newly exposed?** A precise group, not “everyone.”
3. **Why does the old approach fail?** A structural explanation, not feature criticism.
4. **What new principle is required?** The governing idea behind the new category.
5. **What is the category?** A short name plus a one-sentence definition.
6. **Why now?** Evidence that postponement carries a growing cost.

This structure aligns with *Play Bigger*’s emphasis on defining the problem and framing the category ([Play Bigger](https://www.playbigger.com/)). It also separates category creation from novelty theater: the category exists to improve customer understanding and choice.

### Name for comprehension, not grandeur

Generate names across three patterns:

- **descriptive:** says what the category does;
- **analogy/bridge:** borrows a known mental model and adds a modifier;
- **new term:** creates a fresh label when existing language actively misleads.

Score candidates on comprehension, memorability, pronounceability, extensibility, distinctiveness, and fit with the customer’s own language. Test names in blind interviews: “What would you expect this category to do?”, “Who is it for?”, and “What would you compare it with?” Research on brand-name fluency links ease of processing with recall and willingness to buy, reinforcing the case for simple, pronounceable language ([Journal of Promotion Management](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10496491.2014.946203)).

Do not force a coined term if an adjacent category gets the audience to understanding faster. Dunford treats category as context for value, not as a mandatory declaration of uniqueness ([April Dunford](https://www.aprildunford.com/post/a-quickstart-guide-to-positioning)). A useful bridge can be “a [familiar category] for [new job]” until evidence supports a standalone label.

### Build a category narrative

Use a repeatable sequence:

- **Old world:** the accepted practice and assumption.
- **Change:** what makes the assumption obsolete.
- **Problem:** the newly visible cost or risk.
- **New world:** the principle that resolves it.
- **Category:** the name and definition.
- **Product:** the leading implementation, not the whole category.
- **Proof and action:** evidence plus the next step.

Category-first storytelling should still connect to concrete outcomes. HBR’s distinction between first movers and category creators implies that the category claim must extend beyond novelty to a coherent market frame ([Harvard Business Review](https://hbr.org/2019/11/the-difference-between-a-first-mover-and-a-category-creator)).

### Design a “category creation moment” carefully

A manifesto, benchmark report, live demonstration, named methodology, event, or public challenge can concentrate attention around the new POV. Make the moment newsworthy through evidence or consequence rather than self-description. The goal is not a one-day launch spike; it is to seed reusable language that customers, creators, journalists, and analysts repeat.

### Exit criterion

Advance when the name reduces explanation time, does not create misleading expectations, and target customers begin using the problem or category language spontaneously. Keep the name provisional until repeated external use demonstrates that it helps.

## 5. Step 4 — Pick a beachhead and ICP

A new category rarely becomes obvious to the whole market at once. Geoffrey Moore’s *Crossing the Chasm* logic concentrates resources on a beachhead where customers share a compelling reason to buy and can reference one another; Moore later describes expansion as moving beyond that beachhead after establishing a position ([Lenny’s Newsletter interview with Geoffrey Moore](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/geoffrey-moore-on-finding-your-beachhead); [Geoffrey Moore](https://geoffreyamoore.com/business_blogs/after-the-chasm-scaling-beyond-the-beachhead/)).

### Score candidate beachheads

Rate each segment from 1–5 on:

- pain intensity and frequency;
- trigger clarity and urgency;
- dissatisfaction with alternatives;
- ease of identification and reach;
- shared language and information sources;
- ability to show value quickly;
- density of peer references or social proof;
- willingness to advocate;
- realistic channel access and acquisition cost.

Choose the smallest segment large enough to learn and build momentum, not the largest theoretical market. Define the ICP behaviorally: **who + circumstance + trigger + current alternative + desired progress + proof required**.

### B2B and B2C application

- **B2B:** start with one use case, role, company context, and triggering event. Create content for champions and for the hidden stakeholders who may veto an unfamiliar category; strong thought leadership can help lesser-known vendors gain advocates inside buying groups ([Edelman–LinkedIn](https://www.edelman.com/expertise/Business-Marketing/2025-b2b-thought-leadership-report)).
- **B2C:** start with one high-intensity occasion or identity-based community rather than a broad demographic. Favor segments where people observe one another’s behavior or naturally share the outcome, because peer communication helps diffuse new products ([research on word of mouth and new-product diffusion](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1462336)).

### Create a segment-specific proof package

The package should include a tailored problem statement, category definition, demonstration, objection handling, customer story or pilot evidence, and one conversion path. Concentration creates faster learning because every response tests the same audience-message-channel combination.

### Exit criterion

Expand only after the beachhead shows repeatable comprehension, conversion, value realization, retention or repeat behavior, and references. Expansion should move to an adjacent segment that shares the core job and can reuse proof—not to a random large audience.

## 6. Step 5 — Brand foundations on a startup budget

Brand is the memory structure that makes the category story recognizable and trustworthy. It is not merely a logo. Sequoia’s story-driven design approach starts with a clear story and uses it to guide the experience, an appropriate discipline for a startup that must teach a new idea consistently ([Sequoia Capital](https://sequoiacap.com/article/story-driven-design)).

### Build the minimum viable brand system

1. **Strategic core:** audience, problem, POV, promise, personality, and proof.
2. **Verbal identity:** company and category naming, one-line descriptor, message hierarchy, vocabulary to use, vocabulary to avoid, and founder narrative.
3. **Visual identity:** a legible wordmark, accessible type and color system, simple image/illustration rules, and reusable templates.
4. **Trust system:** consistent proof formats, customer evidence, methodology, transparent claims, and clear calls to action.
5. **Touchpoint kit:** landing page, social profile, pitch/demo deck, email, content template, press page, and community/event assets.

Storytelling helps people organize information, but the narrative must be meaningful to customers rather than founder-centered; HBR’s brand-story guidance emphasizes connecting the story to the audience and strategic meaning ([Harvard Business Review](https://hbr.org/2013/06/make-your-brand-story-meaningf); [Harvard Business Review](https://hbr.org/2014/03/the-irresistible-power-of-storytelling-as-a-strategic-business-tool)).

### Budget priorities

Spend first on clarity and consistency: positioning, copy, a flexible design system, proof, and templates. Delay expensive campaigns, elaborate brand films, and large redesigns until message and category hypotheses stabilize. Keep category and company distinct: the company should lead the category, but credible categories can be discussed by customers and partners without sounding like a proprietary product feature.

### Exit criterion

A new contributor should be able to produce an on-brand page or post from the kit, and target customers should recognize the company’s point of view across touchpoints. If the system looks consistent but still requires lengthy explanation, improve the strategy before the visuals.

## 7. Step 6 — Select channels with the Bullseye Framework

*Traction* organizes customer acquisition into 19 possible channels and the Bullseye Framework forces founders to consider, test, and then focus rather than defaulting to familiar tactics ([Bullseye Framework worksheet](https://assets.ctfassets.net/pn8wbiqtnzw9/5b7C5LsvXTDELQZPR3Df6W/da1bd4e502202c7fede1dcc263235d39/BullseyeFramework_A4_PDF.pdf); [*Traction* book listing](https://www.amazon.com/Traction-Startup-Achieve-Explosive-Customer/dp/1591848369)).

### Run Bullseye in three rings

1. **Outer ring — enumerate:** consider all plausible channels, including content, PR, search, paid social/search, partnerships, communities, events, outbound, affiliates, referral, viral loops, and offline routes.
2. **Middle ring — test:** select several channels whose mechanics match the beachhead. Run cheap, time-boxed tests with a single audience, message, offer, and success threshold.
3. **Inner ring — focus:** concentrate on the one or two channels that show repeatable qualified response, economic promise, or compounding distribution.

### Design learning tests

For each test, specify:

- hypothesis: “This audience has this problem and will respond to this message in this channel”;
- minimum viable asset and offer;
- fixed budget and duration;
- target segment and exclusions;
- leading metric: qualified reply, signup, scheduled conversation, activated user, referral, or content completion;
- downstream quality metric: activation, retention, repeat behavior, or pipeline progression;
- decision rule: scale, iterate, or stop.

Novel categories often need channels that permit explanation and trust transfer before high-intent demand exists. Founder content, targeted communities, events, podcasts, partnerships, earned media, and direct conversations can outperform keyword capture early because they provide context. Search and paid acquisition become more powerful as category language and demand emerge. This is a hypothesis to test, not a universal rule; the Bullseye discipline exists precisely because founders systematically underexplore channels ([Bullseye Framework worksheet](https://assets.ctfassets.net/pn8wbiqtnzw9/5b7C5LsvXTDELQZPR3Df6W/da1bd4e502202c7fede1dcc263235d39/BullseyeFramework_A4_PDF.pdf)).

### Guard against false positives

A channel can generate cheap clicks while attracting people who misunderstand the category. Judge channels on qualified progression and cohort behavior. Separate **channel failure** from **message failure** by keeping one variable stable where possible. Document negative results so tests do not repeat.

### Exit criterion

Focus when a channel repeatedly reaches the beachhead, produces qualified movement beyond the first click, and has a believable path to scale or compound. Maintain a smaller exploration budget because channel performance changes as category awareness grows.

## 8. Step 7 — Content and thought leadership as category education

For a category creator, content is not a publishing quota; it is the curriculum by which the market learns a new problem, vocabulary, and decision standard. In B2B, the 2025 Edelman–LinkedIn study emphasizes that strong thought leadership can influence visible and hidden buyers and help less familiar vendors gain internal advocates ([Edelman–LinkedIn](https://www.edelman.com/expertise/Business-Marketing/2025-b2b-thought-leadership-report)). Content Marketing Institute’s 2026 research also reports widespread thought-leadership activity but a much smaller share of advanced or leading programs, indicating that distinctiveness and operating discipline—not mere production—are the constraint ([Content Marketing Institute](https://contentmarketinginstitute.com/b2b-research/b2b-content-marketing-trends-research)).

### Build a category-education curriculum

Use six recurring content jobs:

1. **Problem recognition:** diagnostic checklists, symptom explainers, stories, calculators, and “hidden cost” analyses.
2. **Why now:** data and analysis of the change that makes the old approach inadequate.
3. **New mental model:** the category POV, named principles, and a memorable visual framework.
4. **Mechanism education:** demonstrations and plain-language explanations of how the new approach works.
5. **Proof:** benchmarks, customer evidence, experiments, expert interviews, and transparent limitations.
6. **Action:** implementation guides, evaluation criteria, self-assessments, and a low-friction next step.

Create one flagship category asset—such as an original benchmark, field guide, or manifesto—then atomize it into founder posts, short videos, webinars, partner content, email sequences, sales-enablement content, and PR angles. CMI identifies LinkedIn, email newsletters, and speaking/webinars among the most effective reported thought-leadership channels in its 2026 B2B survey ([Content Marketing Institute](https://contentmarketinginstitute.com/b2b-research/b2b-content-marketing-trends-research)). Channel selection should still follow beachhead behavior rather than a generic ranking.

### Make thought leadership genuinely distinctive

A useful piece must do at least one of the following:

- reveal new evidence;
- make a non-obvious argument;
- name and explain a pattern customers recognize;
- provide a practical model or tool;
- show proof that changes perceived risk;
- challenge a default while fairly representing its strengths.

Avoid “category Mad Libs” that repeatedly insert the new term into generic advice. Edelman’s research highlights perspective-shifting ideas as a way to engage hidden buyers ([Edelman–LinkedIn](https://www.edelman.com/expertise/Business-Marketing/2025-b2b-thought-leadership-report)). Distinctiveness should come from evidence and interpretation, not manufactured controversy.

### Measure educational impact

Track qualified consumption, saves/shares by the ICP, newsletter reply quality, branded and category search growth, direct traffic, assisted conversions, invitations, backlink/citation quality, and whether customers repeat the vocabulary. CMI reports that marketers commonly assess thought leadership through audience engagement and business impact such as leads or pipeline influence ([Content Marketing Institute](https://contentmarketinginstitute.com/b2b-research/b2b-content-marketing-trends-research)). For a new category, add **language adoption** and **problem comprehension** as explicit outcomes.

### B2C adaptation

Replace enterprise white papers with demonstrations, creator explanations, customer stories, interactive tools, short-form education, and shareable identity or utility. Preserve the same curriculum: symptom, why now, new model, mechanism, proof, action. Peer credibility matters because electronic word-of-mouth can shape attitudes and intentions ([peer-reviewed review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5524892/)).

## 9. Step 8 — Community, evangelism, and founder-led marketing

### Founder-led marketing

Early in a category’s life, the founder often has the highest concentration of insight, conviction, customer stories, and permission to make a sharp claim. First Round’s founder-led growth playbook describes the advantage of founders staying close to customers and using that proximity to drive growth learning ([First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/founder-led-growth-playbook/)). Convert that advantage into a repeatable marketing system:

- publish the category POV in the founder’s natural voice;
- share customer-derived observations, not motivational generalities;
- document experiments, counterintuitive findings, and changes of mind;
- participate where the beachhead already gathers;
- route replies and objections into the message evidence repository;
- repurpose conversations into FAQ, proof, and content themes.

Founder reach is a bridge, not the brand’s only distribution asset. Build owned email, community relationships, searchable content, partner channels, customer advocacy, and repeatable formats so category education can outlast the founder’s posting cadence.

### Community as a value exchange

Do not launch a community merely to possess an audience. Define the member-to-member value that exists even when the company is quiet: peer learning, status, accountability, access, shared identity, data, or collective problem solving. a16z’s “Social+” thesis argues that social/community dynamics become powerful when attached to a functional product or experience rather than bolted on as an empty social layer ([Andreessen Horowitz](https://a16z.com/community-takes-all-the-power-of-social/)).

A lean community sequence is:

1. participate in existing communities and learn their norms;
2. convene a small recurring ritual around the problem—roundtable, clinic, challenge, benchmark review, or showcase;
3. recognize useful member contributions;
4. invite members to teach, host, or create;
5. connect participation to genuine product or professional value;
6. formalize an advocate program only after organic contribution appears.

### Design an evangelism loop

**Value experienced → story captured → advocate equipped → peer exposed → new member/user experiences value.** Supply advocates with clear language, proof, examples, and easy referral mechanics, but preserve authenticity. Peer-to-peer communication contributes to new-product diffusion, so customer stories should explain the original struggle, trigger, alternative, outcome, and changed belief—not just praise the company ([research on word of mouth and new-product diffusion](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1462336)).

### Product-led growth, where applicable

If value can be experienced before purchase, marketing should connect education to a fast value moment, then make sharing or collaboration a natural by-product. Amplitude’s PLG benchmark guidance emphasizes measuring behavioral conversion and retention rather than treating signups as success ([Amplitude](https://amplitude.com/blog/benchmark-product-led-growth)). PLG is not synonymous with “free”; the product must deliver recognizable value and create an observable path from acquisition to repeated use.

For high-trust, high-price, regulated, or service-heavy products, use guided demos, workshops, pilots, assessments, or concierge onboarding as the experiential equivalent. The marketing principle is the same: compress the distance between category claim and witnessed proof.

## 10. Step 9 — PR and analyst relations for a new category

### Lead with a story, not the existence of the startup

A journalist needs consequence, evidence, conflict, change, novelty, or human relevance. A category announcement without external proof is usually a branding claim. Y Combinator’s press guidance recommends understanding the reporter, making the story easy to grasp, and pitching a genuinely newsworthy angle rather than a generic company update ([Y Combinator](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/getting-press-for-your-startup/); [Y Combinator launch-story guide](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/a-guide-to-pitching-funding-and-launch-stories/)).

Strong category-creation angles include:

- original data revealing an overlooked problem;
- a demonstrable change in customer behavior;
- a timely external shift that makes the old approach fail;
- a counterintuitive trend with credible evidence;
- a vivid customer story illustrating broader stakes;
- an open benchmark, methodology, or challenge others can examine.

Create a press kit with a one-sentence problem statement, category definition, founder and company boilerplates, original evidence and methodology, customer/expert voices, visuals, demo, FAQs, and explicit limitations. Brief under embargo only when there is a real time-bound story. Personalize outreach to a reporter’s beat and explain why the audience should care now.

### Make the category bigger than the company

Journalists are more likely to adopt language that describes a genuine pattern and includes multiple actors. Map adjacent companies, academics, practitioners, customers, and critics. Offer the category thesis as a useful lens, not a demand that coverage crown the startup. Track whether coverage accurately repeats the problem, category definition, and stakes; logo mentions without comprehension are a weak result.

### Analyst relations for B2B

Analyst relations is a long-term learning and credibility program, not a campaign to force placement in a market map. Gartner describes analysts as influential with technology buyers and recommends using interactions to understand market questions and strengthen communication ([Gartner](https://www.gartner.com/en/documents/4021182)). Forrester’s briefing guidance emphasizes preparation, clarity, relevance to the analyst’s coverage, and a substantive exchange rather than a one-way pitch ([Forrester](https://www.forrester.com/blogs/my-tips-for-crushing-your-analyst-briefings-and-wowing-the-analyst/)).

For a category that does not fit an existing taxonomy:

1. identify analysts covering the problem, budget, workflow, and adjacent markets;
2. request an informational briefing with a crisp category thesis and customer evidence;
3. show how buyers describe the problem and what alternatives they use;
4. ask how the analyst frames the space and where the thesis conflicts with observed demand;
5. send meaningful updates—new evidence, adoption patterns, benchmark data—not routine promotional news;
6. preserve honest disagreement and avoid overstating analyst validation.

**B2C difference:** traditional analyst relations is often less important; prioritize consumer media, specialist reviewers, creators, communities, and experts trusted by the beachhead. The same rule applies: transfer credibility through evidence and independent interpretation.

### Exit criterion

PR/AR is working when third parties accurately explain the problem and category, qualified direct traffic and conversations follow, and the category language appears independently. Volume of mentions alone is a vanity metric.

## 11. Step 10 — Measure what matters early

### Build a category-learning funnel

A novel category needs measurement before conventional lead or purchase metrics. Track the sequence:

1. **Reach:** did the beachhead encounter the message?
2. **Problem recognition:** do they identify the problem as relevant?
3. **Comprehension:** can they explain the category and value?
4. **Intent:** do they take a meaningful next step?
5. **Value realization:** do they experience the promised outcome?
6. **Retention/repeat:** do they return, continue, or repurchase?
7. **Advocacy:** do they refer, share, teach, or publicly endorse?

Nielsen argues that long-term brand growth requires attention across the funnel rather than optimization only at the bottom, and separately emphasizes the importance of awareness for later effectiveness ([Nielsen](https://www.nielsen.com/insights/2022/why-focusing-on-the-entire-marketing-funnel-is-key-for-long-term-brand-growth/); [Nielsen](https://www.nielsen.com/insights/2021/when-it-comes-to-brand-building-awareness-is-critical/)). For a resource-constrained startup, this does not mean buying mass awareness; it means measuring whether targeted awareness is becoming understanding and behavior.

### Choose a North Star tied to value

Amplitude defines the North Star as a measure of the value customers receive and connects it to a small set of input metrics the team can influence ([Amplitude North Star Framework](https://amplitude.com/books/north-star/about-north-star-framework)). Reforge similarly cautions that North Star selection depends on the product’s growth model and should connect acquisition, retention, and monetization rather than optimize an isolated count ([Reforge](https://www.reforge.com/blog/north-star-metrics)).

Examples—not universal prescriptions—include:

- B2B workflow: qualified teams completing the core value-generating workflow per week;
- marketplace: successful matches or transactions with quality guardrails;
- consumer subscription: retained users completing the core outcome-bearing action;
- community product: members receiving and contributing useful responses;
- high-touch service: customers reaching a verified milestone within a target time.

Pair the North Star with guardrails for customer quality, retention, satisfaction, concentration, and cost. A rising signup count with weak activation is not progress.

### Early marketing dashboard

| Layer | Core metrics | Diagnostic use |
|---|---|---|
| Category awareness | aided/unaided problem recognition; branded/category search; qualified reach | Is the idea entering memory? |
| Message | comprehension; accurate retell; objection frequency; landing-page qualified conversion | Does the frame make sense? |
| Channel | cost per qualified action; activation by source; cohort retention; assisted conversion | Is distribution reaching the right people? |
| Content | ICP completion; saves/shares; replies; citations/backlinks; influenced conversion | Is education changing understanding? |
| Community/advocacy | active contributors; useful member interactions; referrals; referred-user quality | Is belief spreading peer to peer? |
| PR/AR | message pull-through; qualified direct traffic; independent category usage | Is third-party validation improving credibility? |
| Value | activation/time-to-value; repeat/retention; North Star; advocacy | Does the promise become experienced value? |

### Use cohorts and loops, not only funnels

Aggregate conversion can hide whether marketing quality is improving. Compare cohorts by segment, message, channel, and start date. Reforge’s growth-loop framework shows how outputs of one cycle can become inputs to the next; referrals, user-generated proof, searchable content, integrations, and community contributions can therefore compound rather than reset like one-way campaigns ([Reforge](https://www.reforge.com/blog/growth-loops)). Instrument both the loop’s input and the quality of the returning output.

### Establish an operating cadence

- **Weekly:** review experiments, qualitative feedback, comprehension, qualified actions, and activation.
- **Monthly:** examine cohorts, channel quality, content influence, category language adoption, and proof gaps.
- **Quarterly:** revisit ICP, positioning, category POV, North Star, and channel concentration.

McKinsey argues that modern marketing measurement should triangulate methods rather than depend on one attribution view ([McKinsey & Company](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/growth-marketing-and-sales/our-insights/the-future-of-marketing-measurement)). Early startups can apply the same principle modestly: combine behavioral analytics, cohort analysis, self-reported discovery, customer interviews, and controlled tests instead of pretending last-click attribution is causal truth.

## 12. Common mistakes and anti-patterns

| Anti-pattern | Why it fails | Corrective action |
|---|---|---|
| Declaring “no competition” | Customers always have an alternative, including doing nothing. | Map competitive alternatives first, as Dunford’s positioning process requires ([April Dunford](https://www.aprildunford.com/post/an-introduction-to-positioning)). |
| Naming the category before discovery | Produces founder jargon disconnected from a repeated customer struggle. | Ground the name in behavioral interviews and JTBD circumstances ([Christensen Institute](https://www.christenseninstitute.org/theory/jobs-to-be-done/)). |
| Leading with novelty | “First” does not explain relevance, urgency, or credibility. | Lead with the changed world, problem, new principle, and proof; category creation is not merely first-mover status ([Harvard Business Review](https://hbr.org/2019/11/the-difference-between-a-first-mover-and-a-category-creator)). |
| Trying to educate everyone | Spreads limited reach and proof too thinly. | Win a beachhead that shares a compelling reason to act ([Lenny’s Newsletter with Geoffrey Moore](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/geoffrey-moore-on-finding-your-beachhead)). |
| Category story eclipses the product | Buyers may agree with the thesis yet not know what to do next. | Connect every educational asset to mechanism, proof, and one relevant action. |
| Product story eclipses the problem | Feature lists assume buyers already understand the category. | Match content to awareness stage: symptom → stakes → category → mechanism → proof. |
| Scaling paid acquisition before comprehension | Spend amplifies ambiguity and can optimize toward low-quality clicks. | Establish message-market fit and downstream quality before scale. |
| Using every channel at once | Prevents clean learning and resource concentration. | Use Bullseye tests, then focus on 1–2 channels ([Bullseye Framework](https://assets.ctfassets.net/pn8wbiqtnzw9/5b7C5LsvXTDELQZPR3Df6W/da1bd4e502202c7fede1dcc263235d39/BullseyeFramework_A4_PDF.pdf)). |
| Publishing generic thought leadership | Adds volume without changing the buyer’s mental model. | Publish evidence, a distinctive argument, tools, or perspective-shifting insight ([Edelman–LinkedIn](https://www.edelman.com/expertise/Business-Marketing/2025-b2b-thought-leadership-report)). |
| Launching an empty branded community | Members receive no peer value and participation becomes a content burden. | Begin with an existing group or a small recurring value-rich ritual; attach social interaction to functional value ([a16z](https://a16z.com/community-takes-all-the-power-of-social/)). |
| Treating PR as announcement distribution | Company existence is not inherently newsworthy. | Build stories around evidence, consequence, change, and audience relevance ([Y Combinator](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/getting-press-for-your-startup/)). |
| Seeking analyst “approval” | Encourages overclaiming and wastes a learning relationship. | Brief relevant analysts with evidence, ask for their frame, and report validation precisely ([Forrester](https://www.forrester.com/blogs/dos-and-donts-for-analyst-interactions/)). |
| Measuring followers, impressions, or signups alone | Exposure can rise while comprehension and value remain flat. | Connect reach to comprehension, qualified intent, activation, retention, and advocacy ([Amplitude](https://amplitude.com/books/north-star/about-north-star-framework)). |
| Treating the founder as the only channel | Creates reach concentration and limits institutional learning. | Turn founder insight into owned assets, customer advocacy, partnerships, and repeatable formats ([First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/founder-led-growth-playbook/)). |
| Changing category language constantly | Prevents memory formation and makes evidence incomparable. | Hold the core frame stable during a defined test window; change it only from documented learning. |
| Refusing to change the category label | Turns strategy into ideology when the market signals confusion. | Treat the name as a hypothesis and preserve the customer problem even if the label changes. |

## 13. Suggested 90-day marketing plan

This plan assumes an early-stage company with limited baseline research. It is a template; the pace should reflect sales cycle, purchase risk, and access to customers. Each phase has a learning gate so calendar pressure does not substitute for evidence.

### Days 1–30: Find the truth and frame it

**Objectives:** establish the problem evidence, initial beachhead, positioning hypotheses, and measurement baseline.

**Weeks 1–2**

- Recruit customers and non-customers from 2–3 plausible high-intensity segments.
- Conduct behavior-based Mom Test/JTBD interviews; reconstruct last occurrence, trigger, workaround, consequence, and information journey ([The Mom Test](https://www.momtestbook.com/); [Christensen Institute](https://www.christenseninstitute.org/theory/jobs-to-be-done/)).
- Build the evidence repository and code verbatim language, alternatives, triggers, costs, channels, objections, and proof requirements.
- Inventory existing touchpoints and establish baseline reach, comprehension, qualified actions, activation, and retention.

**Weeks 3–4**

- Score candidate beachheads on pain, urgency, reachability, proof speed, and reference density, following the concentration logic of *Crossing the Chasm* ([Lenny’s Newsletter with Geoffrey Moore](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/geoffrey-moore-on-finding-your-beachhead)).
- Complete Dunford’s positioning sequence: alternatives → differentiated capabilities → enabled value → best-fit customers → market context ([April Dunford](https://www.aprildunford.com/post/a-quickstart-guide-to-positioning)).
- Draft three message frames and test comprehension, retell, contrast, resonance, and behavior with the beachhead.
- Draft the category POV; generate and blind-test category names, including a familiar bridge option.

**Day-30 deliverables**

- interview evidence repository;
- problem/JTBD brief;
- beachhead/ICP scorecard;
- positioning canvas;
- message test results;
- provisional category POV and name shortlist;
- baseline category-learning dashboard.

**Gate:** proceed only if one segment shows repeated consequential behavior and one positioning frame produces clear understanding. Otherwise, continue discovery rather than launch a broad campaign.

### Days 31–60: Build the teaching and distribution system

**Objectives:** encode the narrative, create proof, and identify promising channels.

**Weeks 5–6**

- Finalize a provisional category name and one-sentence definition from test evidence.
- Build the narrative: old world → change → problem → new principle → category → product → proof → action, consistent with category design’s problem-and-frame sequence ([Play Bigger](https://www.playbigger.com/)).
- Create the minimum viable brand kit: verbal rules, accessible visual system, proof formats, landing page, founder/social templates, email, and briefing deck; use a story-first system to preserve coherence ([Sequoia Capital](https://sequoiacap.com/article/story-driven-design)).
- Produce one flagship educational asset and supporting problem, mechanism, proof, and action content.

**Weeks 7–8**

- Populate the Bullseye outer ring, prioritize several plausible channels, and define low-cost tests with fixed decision rules ([Bullseye Framework](https://assets.ctfassets.net/pn8wbiqtnzw9/5b7C5LsvXTDELQZPR3Df6W/da1bd4e502202c7fede1dcc263235d39/BullseyeFramework_A4_PDF.pdf)).
- Run tests with the same beachhead and stable core message; measure qualified progression and activation by source.
- Establish a founder publishing cadence built from customer evidence and the category curriculum; founder proximity to customers should accelerate learning ([First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/founder-led-growth-playbook/)).
- Convene one small value-rich community ritual in an existing community or with a curated cohort; do not launch a large empty forum ([Andreessen Horowitz](https://a16z.com/community-takes-all-the-power-of-social/)).

**Day-60 deliverables**

- provisional category and messaging guide;
- lean brand/touchpoint kit;
- flagship category asset and derivative content;
- channel test scorecards;
- founder-led content system;
- first community ritual and qualitative feedback;
- updated dashboard with message/channel cohorts.

**Gate:** concentrate only if at least one channel produces repeatable qualified movement and the message survives comprehension/retell tests. If not, diagnose audience, message, offer, and channel separately.

### Days 61–90: Concentrate, validate, and compound

**Objectives:** focus distribution, strengthen independent proof, and install a repeatable learning cadence.

**Weeks 9–10**

- Allocate most test resources to the best 1–2 channels while retaining a small exploration lane, consistent with Bullseye focus ([Bullseye Framework](https://assets.ctfassets.net/pn8wbiqtnzw9/5b7C5LsvXTDELQZPR3Df6W/da1bd4e502202c7fede1dcc263235d39/BullseyeFramework_A4_PDF.pdf)).
- Create segment-specific proof: customer story, benchmark, demonstration, expert evidence, or before/after process.
- Invite successful customers/users to tell struggle-to-outcome stories and equip them with accurate category language; peer communication supports diffusion ([new-product word-of-mouth research](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1462336)).
- Improve the path from content or community to value realization; measure time-to-value and retained behavior, not signup volume.

**Weeks 11–12**

- Pitch a small, relevant set of journalists or creators with an evidence-led story tailored to their audience; follow YC’s emphasis on relevance and newsworthiness ([Y Combinator](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/getting-press-for-your-startup/)).
- For B2B, brief analysts covering the problem and adjacent markets; present buyer evidence, ask for their frame, and record taxonomy objections ([Gartner](https://www.gartner.com/en/documents/4021182); [Forrester](https://www.forrester.com/blogs/my-tips-for-crushing-your-analyst-briefings-and-wowing-the-analyst/)).
- Choose a customer-value North Star and input metrics; Amplitude’s framework requires that the measure represent value received, not just activity ([Amplitude](https://amplitude.com/books/north-star/about-north-star-framework)).
- Run the 90-day review: compare cohorts; assess problem recognition, comprehension, language adoption, qualified intent, value realization, retention, advocacy, and channel quality.

**Day-90 deliverables**

- focused 1–2-channel operating plan;
- proof library and advocate loop;
- PR/creator/analyst briefing kit;
- North Star and guardrails;
- 90-day evidence review;
- decision memo: keep, refine, bridge, or change the category language.

**Gate for the next quarter:** scale only the audience-message-channel combinations that lead to realized customer value. Retain the problem thesis if evidence supports it, even if the category name or channel mix must change.

## Closing principle

A new category is not created when a startup coins a term. It is created when a specific audience adopts a new way of understanding a consequential problem, uses that understanding to evaluate choices, experiences the promised value, and repeats the idea to others. The marketing system should therefore optimize for **shared belief supported by proof**: discovery finds the truth, positioning makes it legible, category design makes it memorable, a beachhead makes it winnable, channels make it reachable, content makes it teachable, community and earned validation make it credible, and measurement keeps the story accountable.
