# Marketing Playbook for a Commoditized Business

## Executive summary

A commoditized business does not need to teach people why the category exists. Buyers already know what pizza is, what a yoga mat does, and where alternatives can be found. The marketing problem is therefore not category creation. It is to become **easy to notice, easy to remember in relevant buying situations, easy to buy, and easy to choose again**. In Ehrenberg-Bass terms, growth comes from expanding mental and physical availability; category entry points are the cues that bring a buyer into the market and create the situations in which a brand must be recalled ([Ehrenberg-Bass Institute](https://marketingscience.info/learn-with-us/commercial-research/identifying-and-prioritising-category-entry-points)).

This reframes the founder’s job:

- **Demand already exists.** Capture it where buyers search, browse, compare, and purchase.
- **Functional quality is necessary but rarely self-distributing.** Convert quality into visible, specific proof: reviews, demonstrations, repeat behavior, guarantees, and recommendations.
- **Distinctiveness usually travels farther than a long list of minor differences.** Consistent colors, shapes, packaging, phrases, sounds, and experiences help buyers identify the brand quickly; Ehrenberg-Bass research treats distinctive assets as devices that make a brand uniquely recognizable ([Ehrenberg-Bass Institute](https://marketingscience.info/news-and-insights/brands-need-distinctive-assets)).
- **Penetration and availability matter.** Small brands generally have fewer buyers and slightly lower loyalty—the empirical “double jeopardy” pattern—so growth cannot rely only on making a small customer base intensely loyal ([Ehrenberg-Bass Institute](https://marketingscience.info/news-and-insights/effective-brand-growth-acquisition-or-retention)).
- **Distribution is marketing.** A pizza shop’s map visibility, hours, ordering flow, delivery radius, and review profile are physical and digital availability. A yoga-mat brand’s Amazon listing, retail presence, inventory, product page, shipping promise, and returns are the equivalent.
- **Paid channels are auctions, not permanent advantages.** More competition can make the next customer more expensive. Creative quality, conversion rate, repeat purchase, organic demand, and channel diversification are the practical defenses.
- **Brand and activation must work together.** Binet and Field’s analysis of the IPA effectiveness database argues that sales activation produces nearer-term response while brand building creates broader, longer-lived demand effects; the famous 60/40 split is a starting heuristic, not a law for every business ([Marketing Week](https://www.marketingweek.com/this-much-i-learned-les-binet-peter-field/); [Ehrenberg-Bass/Marketing Science](https://marketingscience.info/mark-ritson-binet-and-fields-research-may-not-be-perfect-but-that-doesnt-make-it-wrong/)).

The strategic sequence is: verify the experience; encode a recognizable brand; position it in the existing category; choose initial customers and occasions; maximize availability; acquire demand without losing unit economics; compound reviews, content, partners, retention, and word of mouth; then measure incremental profit rather than platform-reported activity.

## 1. The real problem: growth when buyers already have choices

### Mental availability, physical availability, and penetration

In a crowded category, the competitive bottleneck is usually not awareness of the problem. It is **retrieval and access**. Mental availability is the probability that a buyer notices or thinks of a brand in a buying situation; physical availability is the probability that the brand can be found and bought. Category entry points (CEPs)—such as “Friday night, nobody wants to cook” or “I need a mat that will not slip in a heated class”—are the cues around which memory structures can be built ([Ehrenberg-Bass Institute](https://marketingscience.info/learn-with-us/commercial-research/identifying-and-prioritising-category-entry-points)).

This is a more useful growth model than “convince everyone we are objectively best.” Buyers are often light, infrequent category purchasers making low-attention choices. Small brands typically suffer “double jeopardy”: fewer people buy them, and those buyers are somewhat less loyal. Ehrenberg-Bass therefore argues that effective growth requires customer acquisition and broad reach, not only extracting more purchases from existing heavy users ([Ehrenberg-Bass Institute](https://marketingscience.info/news-and-insights/effective-brand-growth-acquisition-or-retention); [Ehrenberg-Bass Institute](https://marketingscience.info/news-and-insights/the-double-jeopardy-law-in-b2b-shows-the-way-to-grow)).

Retention still matters economically; the correction is to avoid treating loyalty as a substitute for reach. A pizza shop needs regulars **and** a continuing flow of nearby occasional buyers. A yoga-mat brand needs satisfied owners and continuing penetration among first-time and replacement buyers. Kantar’s framework adds a useful counterweight: growth is associated not only with salience but with being meaningful and different in ways consumers value, so founders should combine recognizability with a credible preference reason rather than choose one school dogmatically ([Kantar](https://www.kantar.com/inspiration/brands/what-is-the-meaningful-different-salient-framework); [Kantar](https://www.kantar.com/north-america/inspiration/brands/brands-need-to-build-more-than-just-salience-to-grow)).

### Distinctiveness is not the same as differentiation

**Differentiation** answers, “Why prefer this offer?” **Distinctiveness** answers, “How do I know this is the offer?” A claim such as “delicious pizza” or “premium yoga mat” is weak because competitors can make it. A recognizable red-and-cream box, repeated verbal cue, proprietary product photography style, and familiar delivery experience can still make a parity product easier to identify. Mark Ritson’s synthesis is pragmatic: differentiation need not mean literal uniqueness, while distinctiveness and differentiation can reinforce each other ([Marketing Week](https://www.marketingweek.com/ritson-differentiation-uniqueness/); [Marketing Week](https://www.marketingweek.com/ritson-snickers-balanced-differentiation-distinctiveness/)).

The operating goal is therefore **credible difference plus unmistakable identity plus superior access**. A narrow promise gets the first trial; reliable execution validates it; distinctive assets make it retrievable; distribution captures the purchase; retention and advocacy improve the economics.

## 2. Step 1 — Nail the product and experience truth first

Marketing can amplify an experience, but amplification works in both directions. The first task is not to add adjectives; it is to identify what customers consistently experience and can credibly repeat.

### Establish a proof baseline

Measure the customer journey before scaling reach:

1. **Experience reliability:** order accuracy, delivery/pickup time, stockouts, defects, return reasons, complaints, refund rate, and customer-service resolution.
2. **Perceived value:** post-purchase rating, open-ended “why,” review themes, and comparison with the alternative.
3. **Behavior:** first-to-second-purchase rate, repeat interval, cancellation/return rate, referral behavior, and cohort contribution margin.
4. **Advocacy:** willingness to recommend plus the stated reason. Bain’s Net Promoter System sorts customers into promoters, passives, and detractors, but its economics depend on connecting those categories to actual behavior and profitability rather than celebrating a score in isolation ([Bain](https://www.bain.com/insights/the-economics-of-loyalty/); [Harvard Business Review](https://hbr.org/2021/11/net-promoter-3-0)).

NPS is a diagnostic, not the strategy. Pair it with repeat behavior, review text, complaint categories, and unit economics. Academic and practitioner criticism of single-number customer metrics is a reason to triangulate, not a reason to ignore customer feedback.

### Translate quality into a demonstrable “experience truth”

Use specific, observable evidence:

- **Pizza:** “fermented 48 hours,” “ready for pickup in 18 minutes at median,” “crispy after a 15-minute drive,” or “the neighborhood’s most-mentioned pepperoni crust.” Validate operational claims continuously.
- **Yoga mat:** laboratory or controlled demonstrations of grip, thickness and weight specifications, cleaning instructions, material disclosures, wear tests, return reasons, and verified-owner demonstrations.

“Great” is table stakes; a repeatable proof is marketable. The proof must also match the buying occasion. Speed matters during a rushed family dinner; slice portability matters at lunch; grip and cleaning matter in hot yoga; cushioning may matter more for home mobility practice.

### Gate 1

Do not scale acquisition until:

- severe experience failures are bounded and declining;
- the promise can be supported with customer evidence;
- landing-page or listing claims match delivered reality;
- unit contribution is known by order or SKU;
- at least one cohort or customer group demonstrates repeat purchase, recommendation, or low return behavior.

This is a marketing gate, not a product-roadmap detour: paid reach applied to weak reviews and low repeat creates more expensive negative evidence.

## 3. Step 2 — Build distinctiveness before adding more messages

Distinctive assets can include names, logos, colors, type, characters, shapes, packaging, sonic cues, slogans, and recurring visual compositions. Ehrenberg-Bass advises brands to assess and strengthen assets that are both famous (recognized) and unique (correctly linked to the brand), rather than assuming the founder’s favorite color or logo is already distinctive ([Ehrenberg-Bass Institute](https://marketingscience.info/news-and-insights/brands-need-distinctive-assets); [Jenni Romaniuk](http://www.jenniromaniuk.com/)).

### Create a small, disciplined asset system

Choose:

- one dominant color combination that is usable in-store, in feeds, and at thumbnail size;
- one recognizable wordmark or symbol;
- one verbal device: phrase, naming pattern, tone, or mnemonic;
- one packaging or presentation cue;
- one photographic/video grammar;
- one repeatable service ritual or product demonstration.

Then deploy the system across the highest-frequency touchpoints. For pizza: sign, box, menu, employee apparel, delivery-app thumbnail, Google photos, order confirmation, and social posts. For yoga mats: product surface or mark, wrap, listing hero image, thumbnails, shipping box, instruction card, creator brief, emails, and retail display.

### Test assets, do not merely approve them

Show each asset without the brand name and ask category buyers:

1. Which brand does this belong to?
2. How quickly did you recognize it?
3. Does another brand come to mind first?

Retain assets that achieve both recognition and correct attribution. Change them slowly: constant redesign destroys accumulated memory. Ipsos research reported by *Marketing Week* found that only a minority of tested assets were truly distinctive, underscoring the need for measurement rather than internal confidence ([Marketing Week](https://www.marketingweek.com/15-brand-assets-truly-distinctive-finds-research/)).

### Distinctiveness checklist

- Recognizable in two seconds and at mobile-thumbnail size.
- Usable without relying on a long brand name.
- Different enough from category leaders to avoid misattribution.
- Repeated consistently for years, not weeks.
- Flexible across storefront, delivery, marketplace, paid ad, and owned media.
- Attached to the same core promise and CEPs.

## 4. Step 3 — Position inside the existing category

Do not rename pizza or invent “post-mat movement surfaces.” The familiar category is an advantage: it gives buyers language, expectations, and active demand. Positioning should help a chosen buyer understand why this particular option is a strong fit within that frame.

April Dunford defines positioning through five connected components: competitive alternatives, differentiated capabilities, customer value, target customers, and the market category that makes the value obvious. Crucially, the process begins with what customers would use instead and chooses the category context last ([April Dunford](https://www.aprildunford.com/post/an-introduction-to-positioning); [April Dunford](https://www.aprildunford.com/post/a-quickstart-guide-to-positioning)).

### Use a commodity-positioning canvas

1. **Competitive set:** What appears beside the offer at decision time? For pizza, this may include nearby pizzerias, frozen pizza, burgers, cooking, and delivery-app promotions. For mats, it includes low-cost marketplace mats, studio rentals, established athletic brands, and “keep using the old one.”
2. **Provable attribute:** What is consistently better, more convenient, more specific, or more trustworthy?
3. **Value:** Why does that attribute matter in the chosen situation?
4. **Best-fit customer and occasion:** Who cares most, and when?
5. **Frame of reference:** Keep the familiar category, then add a clarifying modifier.
6. **Proof:** Review theme, demonstration, ingredient/material fact, guarantee, usage evidence, or comparison.

A practical template is:

> For **[buyer]** in **[occasion]**, **[brand]** is the **[familiar category]** that delivers **[valuable outcome]** because **[proof/mechanism]**, unlike **[relevant alternative]**.

Examples:

- “For nearby families solving Friday dinner, Hearthline is the neighborhood pizza that arrives crisp and on time, backed by our vented box, pickup-time tracking, and local reviews.”
- “For newer hot-yoga students, Grounded is the easy-care yoga mat that stays stable through sweaty classes, demonstrated in real class conditions and backed by a 60-day trial.”

Avoid unsupported superlatives, generic lifestyle poetry, and ten-attribute propositions. Positioning must be narrow enough to guide media and broad enough to admit future buyers. HBR’s brand-positioning guidance similarly emphasizes a defined target, frame of reference, benefit, and reason to believe ([Harvard Business School Online](https://online.hbs.edu/blog/post/brand-positioning-statement)).

## 5. Step 4 — Pick a beachhead customer and buying occasion

A beachhead in a commodity market is not a forever exclusion. It is the first dense pocket in which message, proof, channel, and word of mouth can reinforce one another.

### Segment by category entry point, not demographics alone

CEPs capture the thoughts and situations that move people into a category purchase ([Ehrenberg-Bass Institute](https://marketingscience.info/learn-with-us/commercial-research/identifying-and-prioritising-category-entry-points)). Build a map:

| Dimension | Pizza examples | Yoga-mat examples |
|---|---|---|
| When | Friday dinner, late night, lunch, game day | First studio class, home practice, replacement, travel |
| Where | Home, office, brewery, school event | Hot studio, living room, outdoors, travel |
| With whom | Family, coworkers, team, friends | Solo, instructor, class, gift recipient |
| Motivation | Convenience, treat, social sharing, value | Stability, comfort, identity, hygiene, portability |
| Friction | Delivery time, sogginess, group preferences | Slipping, odor, weight, confusing materials |

Score potential beachheads on demand frequency, margin, reachability, proof fit, competitive intensity, referral density, and operational ability to fulfill the promise. Pick one primary and one secondary CEP for the first 90 days.

### Preserve reach while concentrating the message

The media audience should not be so narrow that growth stalls. Use the beachhead to sharpen creative and placement, then reach broadly within the set of plausible category buyers. This balances Dunford’s “best-fit customer” logic with the Ehrenberg-Bass emphasis on penetration and light buyers ([April Dunford](https://www.aprildunford.com/post/a-quickstart-guide-to-positioning); [Ehrenberg-Bass Institute](https://marketingscience.info/news-and-insights/effective-brand-growth-acquisition-or-retention)).

For the pizza shop, geography is a hard boundary and occasion is the creative lens. For the yoga-mat brand, shipping expands geography, but customer economics, creator communities, keyword intent, and marketplace competition create the practical boundary.

## 6. Step 5 — Win physical and digital availability

Availability is not a back-office concern. Every extra step, closed channel, missing product, inaccurate listing, slow page, or unclear delivery promise lowers the probability of purchase.

### Local service or retail: own the immediate decision surface

Google states that local results are based mainly on relevance, distance, and prominence, and recommends complete information, verified locations, accurate hours, review responses, and photos ([Google Business Profile Help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091?hl=en)). For a pizza shop:

- fully complete and verify the Google Business Profile;
- use the correct primary category and accurate menu, price, attributes, holiday hours, phone, website, and ordering links;
- publish current exterior, interior, food, team, parking, and pickup photos;
- maintain name, address, and phone consistency across the website and major directories;
- create fast, indexable pages for menu, ordering, catering, delivery area, location, and high-value occasions;
- add local structured data where appropriate and follow Google’s general guidance to make pages helpful, accessible, and understandable to search engines ([Google Search Central](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/seo-starter-guide));
- remove ordering friction: clear ETA, fees, minimums, pickup directions, and accepted payment methods;
- treat delivery apps as rented shelf space—use them for discovery while measuring commissions, discounts, customer ownership, and incremental order contribution.

Moz’s local-search framework likewise emphasizes proximity, profile signals, links, citations, reviews, and behavioral factors; founders should use it as a prioritization model rather than a promise that any single tactic guarantees rank ([Moz](https://moz.com/learn/seo/local-ranking-factors)).

### Physical consumer product: win the digital and retail shelf

For a yoga mat:

- **DTC site:** create fast product pages with clear titles, specifications, comparison information, authentic reviews, shipping/returns, care guidance, structured product data, and original imagery. Google’s ecommerce documentation explains how site structure and product data help search systems understand and surface products ([Google Search Central](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/specialty/ecommerce); [Shopify](https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/promoting-marketing/seo/seo-overview)).
- **Amazon:** optimize category-correct titles, bullets, images, attributes, and backend search terms. Amazon explains that search terms enable lexical matching between a product and a customer query and recommends synonyms and nonduplicative terms while prohibiting misleading or temporary claims ([Amazon Seller Central](https://sellercentral.amazon.com/help/hub/reference/external/G23501?locale=en-US)). Eligible brands can use A+ Content to add richer images, comparison modules, and brand information on detail pages ([Amazon](https://sell.amazon.com/tools/a-content)).
- **Retail or studio:** evaluate doors not by count alone but by sell-through, geographic fit, display quality, inventory turns, and whether staff or instructors can explain the proof.
- **Free product discovery:** maintain accurate feeds for merchant surfaces and consistent price, inventory, shipping, and return information.

### Availability dashboard

Track: branded and nonbranded search visibility, map actions, calls, direction requests, order-start-to-completion rate, listing sessions, marketplace conversion, in-stock rate, delivery promise, retail sell-through, cancellation, and lost sales from stockouts or closed hours.

## 7. Step 6 — Use paid acquisition without surrendering to auction economics

Google calculates Ad Rank whenever an ad is eligible to appear and considers bid, ad and landing-page quality, competition, context, and expected impact of assets; higher quality can support better position and lower cost ([Google Ads Help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/1722122?hl=en); [Google Ads Help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/156066?hl=en)). This makes paid media a competitive auction whose economics can deteriorate as more advertisers pursue the same buyer.

### Channel roles

| Channel | Best role | Pizza shop | Yoga-mat brand |
|---|---|---|---|
| Google Search/Maps | Capture explicit intent | “pizza near me,” catering, delivery | “non-slip hot yoga mat,” brand terms |
| Shopping/marketplaces | Capture product comparison | Usually limited | High-intent product discovery |
| Meta/Instagram | Generate and retarget demand with visual proof | Radius-based occasions, menu, social proof | Demonstrations, creators, use cases |
| TikTok/short video | Demonstrate and create cultural relevance | Making process, reactions, local personality | Grip tests, routines, cleaning, instructors |
| Delivery apps | Rented high-intent distribution | New-user discovery and convenience | Not applicable |
| Retail media/Amazon ads | Win marketplace shelf | Not applicable | Sponsored products, branded defense, category terms |

### A disciplined test design

For every campaign, define:

- audience and buying occasion;
- one promise and one proof;
- offer and landing destination;
- fully loaded spend ceiling;
- primary economic outcome;
- quality guardrail such as new-customer rate, margin, repeat, or return rate;
- scale, revise, and stop thresholds.

Start with high-intent demand capture, then add prospecting where creative can demonstrate the advantage. Separate brand search from nonbrand search; new from returning customers; prospecting from retargeting; discount-led from full-price; and platform-attributed from incremental outcomes. a16z recommends separating paid CAC from blended CAC because blended acquisition can conceal weak paid-channel economics ([Andreessen Horowitz](https://a16z.com/16-startup-metrics/)).

### Creative is an economic lever

In crowded feeds, targeting alone rarely creates durable advantage. Build creative around CEP × proof combinations:

- pizza: family relief, late-night indulgence, lunch speed, game-day sharing;
- mat: sweaty grip, beginner confidence, small-space storage, cleaning ease, joint comfort.

Create multiple executions, but preserve brand assets. Meta recommends creative diversification to give its delivery system varied inputs; diversification should mean genuinely different concepts and formats, not superficial edits to the same ad ([Meta for Business](https://www.facebook.com/business/news/demystifying-creative-diversification)). On search, align keyword, ad, offer, and landing page to improve relevance and conversion.

### Bid to value, not cheap events

Optimize toward contribution-bearing outcomes whenever data volume permits. Google’s value-based bidding guidance supports assigning different conversion values and optimizing for conversion value rather than treating every action as equal ([Google Ads Help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/15099424?hl=en)). A catering lead is not equal to a coupon click; a full-price mat buyer is not equal to a high-return discount buyer.

Pause scaling when marginal CAC exceeds expected gross profit within the acceptable payback window. Do not defend spend because average historical ROAS looks healthy: auction costs and audience quality change at the margin.

## 8. Step 7 — Make reviews, social proof, and UGC a system

Reviews are both persuasion and, for local businesses, part of prominence and discovery. Google explicitly recommends responding to reviews and notes that more reviews and positive ratings can help local ranking ([Google Business Profile Help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091?hl=en)). BrightLocal’s 2026 U.S. survey found that consumers consider rating, recency, volume, consistency, and owner response; 74% cared only about reviews from the prior three months, and 31% said they would only use businesses rated at least 4.5 stars ([BrightLocal](https://www.brightlocal.com/research/local-consumer-review-survey/)).

The revenue evidence is particularly relevant to independent restaurants. Michael Luca’s HBS study found that a one-star Yelp rating increase was associated with a 5%–9% revenue increase and that the effect was driven by independent rather than chain restaurants ([Harvard Business School](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=41233)). The exact effect should not be transplanted to every city or category, but it demonstrates why reputation can materially change local choice.

### Build an ethical review loop

1. Trigger a request after confirmed fulfillment and enough time to experience the product.
2. Make the request frictionless with a direct link or QR code.
3. Ask all eligible customers consistently; do not gate requests based on predicted sentiment.
4. Respond quickly and specifically, especially to negative feedback.
5. Tag review themes and route operational issues to the right owner.
6. Obtain permission before reusing customer text, images, or video in marketing.
7. Monitor review velocity, recency, rating distribution, response time, and recurring issue themes.

For pizza, put the request in the digital receipt, post-order message, and box insert without interrupting the meal. For yoga mats, time it after several uses, then ask separately for a photo or short demonstration. UGC briefs should prompt evidence (“show the surface after a hot class”) rather than generic praise.

### Use social proof by decision stage

- **Discovery:** rating, review count, customer faces, recognizable local or studio context.
- **Evaluation:** detailed quotes, comparison, dimensions, process, creator demonstration.
- **Risk reduction:** guarantees, return policy, service recovery, verified-purchase labels.
- **Reassurance after purchase:** setup, care, usage ideas, community examples.

Online-review research finds that review valence, volume, variance, product type, and platform conditions affect sales rather than producing one universal effect size ([Journal of Retailing meta-analysis](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022435914000293)). Treat proof as a portfolio, not a single star average.

## 9. Step 8 — Use content, SEO, and organic social to help people choose

A commodity brand rarely needs a grand thought-leadership engine. It needs **decision content, usage content, proof content, and repeatable entertainment** tied to buying occasions.

### Search content

Create pages only where the business can provide original utility:

- **Pizza:** menu and ingredient pages, delivery-area pages, catering quantities, allergen guidance, reheating instructions, neighborhood event tie-ins, group-order planning, and occasion pages.
- **Yoga mat:** material and thickness explainers, use-case comparisons, cleaning and storage, class-specific guidance, size/weight charts, beginner routines, and honest product comparisons.

Google’s SEO starter guide emphasizes helpful, well-organized content, descriptive URLs, clear titles, quality images, and links that help users and search systems understand pages ([Google Search Central](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/seo-starter-guide)). Shopify similarly recommends product-page optimization around useful descriptions, images, metadata, and internal linking rather than thin manufacturer copy ([Shopify](https://www.shopify.com/blog/ecommerce-product-page-seo)).

### Organic social

Build recurring series, not random posting:

- **Proof:** dough process, oven exit, delivery test, grip test, cleaning test.
- **People:** maker, cashier, driver, instructor, customer, local partner.
- **Occasion:** Friday dinner, office lunch, first class, travel practice.
- **Participation:** polls, topping combinations, pose challenges, customer remixes.
- **Distinctive ritual:** a recognizable closing shot, phrase, sound, or visual.

Short-form video is useful because the category is inherently demonstrable. HubSpot’s video research reports that short-form video remains a widely used and high-ROI format among surveyed marketers, though self-reported marketer surveys should guide testing rather than substitute for the brand’s own economics ([HubSpot](https://blog.hubspot.com/marketing/video-marketing-statistics)).

### Content operating rule

Every asset should do at least one job: increase retrieval for a CEP, answer a purchase objection, show proof, create reusable ad creative, support a partner, or encourage repeat behavior. Measure qualified search impressions, map/listing actions, saves, completion, assisted conversion, branded search, and reusable winning concepts—not posting frequency alone. Content Marketing Institute’s research repeatedly finds that mature programs depend on strategy and repeatable operations, not merely producing more assets ([Content Marketing Institute](https://contentmarketinginstitute.com/research/b2c-research)).

## 10. Step 9 — Borrow trust and distribution through creators, affiliates, and partners

Partnerships work when there is audience fit, credible context, and measurable incremental access. Large follower counts are not the objective. HBR’s analysis of influencer campaigns found that follower count, posting frequency, brand fit, originality, and other execution choices affect ROI, while subsequent research summarized by HBR/MIT Sloan Management Review found that smaller creators can deliver higher conversion rates than more prominent influencers ([Harvard Business Review](https://hbr.org/2022/11/does-influencer-marketing-really-pay-off); [HBR Store/MIT Sloan Management Review](https://store.hbr.org/product/the-surprising-roi-of-small-online-influencers/SR0256)).

### Local partnership model

For pizza, partner with businesses that already aggregate the relevant occasion:

- breweries, music venues, sports leagues, schools, apartments, hotels, offices, event planners, and community organizations;
- co-created menu items or event bundles;
- tracked catering or group-order links;
- neighborhood creator tastings with clear disclosure and creative freedom;
- recurring events that create memory, not one-off logo swaps.

The partner’s audience, occasion, and geography should overlap with the shop’s service area. Measure new customers, incremental orders, contribution after discounts, and repeat—not coupon redemptions alone.

### Product partnership model

For yoga mats:

- instructors and studio ambassadors who can demonstrate use repeatedly;
- studios as sampling, trial, wholesale, or affiliate partners;
- complementary brands such as towels, activewear, hydration, or wellness travel;
- specialist creators by practice type rather than generic fitness fame;
- affiliates paid on validated sales, with rules for discounting, brand bidding, disclosure, and returns.

Give partners a proof kit—claims, specifications, demo guidance, prohibited language, distinctive assets, tracking links, and disclosure requirements—without scripting away authenticity. Evaluate holdout geographies, codes, post-purchase “how did you hear about us?” data, new-customer mix, returns, and cohort value because last-click affiliate credit can overstate causality.

## 11. Step 10 — Build retention and word of mouth without hiding from penetration

Retention improves the amount the business can afford to spend on acquisition, but it must be measured by cohort profit. Bain’s often-cited research argues that a 5% increase in retention can increase profits 25%–95% in the studied contexts because acquisition costs are front-loaded and longer relationships can produce more purchases and referrals ([Bain](https://www.bain.com/contentassets/29f74ec417fa4e36a1d7d7e7479badc5/loyalty_rules_chapter_one.pdf); [Harvard Business Review](https://hbr.org/2014/10/the-value-of-keeping-the-right-customers)). This range is not a universal forecast; founders should model their own frequency, margin, and churn.

### Design lifecycle marketing around the natural clock

**Pizza**

- capture permission at checkout without obstructing the order;
- send useful, frequency-capped reminders around the customer’s demonstrated occasion;
- support one-tap reorder and saved favorites;
- create event-based triggers for birthdays, game days, school nights, and catering;
- use service recovery quickly after a bad order;
- reward behavior that creates incremental frequency or referral, not purchases that would happen anyway.

**Yoga mats**

- post-purchase sequence: setup, care, class guidance, community proof;
- request a review after real use;
- cross-sell genuinely complementary items;
- time replacement education to observed wear and use, not arbitrary urgency;
- use replenishment only for consumables; a durable mat should not be forced into a monthly-repeat model;
- segment by practice and purchase intent so emails remain relevant.

### Referrals and loyalty

Nielsen has repeatedly found recommendations from people known to the consumer among the most trusted forms of commercial information ([Nielsen](https://www.nielsen.com/news-center/2015/still-recommended-by-friends-and-relatives-the-most-authentic-advertising-according-to-consumers-the-most-trusted-on-brand-websites/)). Wharton research found that referred customers in the studied setting had higher margins, retention, and lifetime value than otherwise comparable nonreferred customers, supporting carefully measured referral programs ([Wharton referral-program study](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2012/04/Schmitt-Skiera-vandenBulte-2011-Referral-Programs-Customer-Value.pdf)).

Use a two-sided referral reward only when it preserves contribution and attracts suitable customers. Loyalty programs should reinforce memory and convenience, not become permanent discount machinery. Track incremental visits, redemption liability, referred-customer quality, breakage, and cannibalization.

## 12. Step 11 — Balance brand building and performance activation

Sales activation converts people likely to buy now; brand building increases the number and ease of future buyers. Binet and Field’s work from the IPA effectiveness database found that longer campaigns produce more broad business effects and popularized an approximate 60% brand-building / 40% activation allocation for many B2C contexts ([Marketing Week](https://www.marketingweek.com/this-much-i-learned-les-binet-peter-field/)). Nielsen likewise argues that concentrating exclusively at the bottom of the funnel can weaken long-term growth and recommends full-funnel measurement ([Nielsen](https://www.nielsen.com/insights/2022/why-focusing-on-the-entire-marketing-funnel-is-key-for-long-term-brand-growth/)).

Treat 60/40 as a planning hypothesis, not a commandment. The correct split varies with maturity, margin, category purchase frequency, geography, cash constraints, distribution, and creative quality. Mark Ritson’s defense of the Binet–Field work also stresses applying the findings with context rather than turning an empirical average into dogma ([Ehrenberg-Bass/Marketing Science](https://marketingscience.info/mark-ritson-binet-and-fields-research-may-not-be-perfect-but-that-doesnt-make-it-wrong/)).

### Practical allocation by archetype

**Pizza shop:** Activation may initially dominate because geography and immediate intent make local search, Maps, delivery apps, and offers highly accountable. Brand building is still required: consistent storefront and box assets, local fame, broad-radius video, sponsorships, and memorable community activity make the restaurant more likely to be chosen before the query begins.

**Yoga-mat brand:** Marketplace and paid-social conversion can drive initial sales, but a durable product has a long replacement cycle. Broader reach, distinctive creative, creator demonstrations, retail exposure, and owned audience development are needed to replenish the buyer pool and avoid perpetual dependence on retargeting.

Use two budgets and two scorecards:

- **Activation:** incremental orders/revenue, CAC, payback, contribution, search impression share.
- **Brand:** reach among category buyers, branded search, direct traffic, asset recognition, CEP association, new-customer penetration, and long-term geo/cohort lift.

Do not demand that every brand impression close a sale immediately, and do not excuse brand spending from commercial evaluation.

## 13. Step 12 — Measure profit, incrementality, and payback

Platform dashboards report attributed events; management needs incremental economics. Google describes incrementality testing as comparing an exposed treatment group with a control group to estimate outcomes caused by advertising, and its geo-based Conversion Lift tools apply that logic across regions ([Google Think](https://business.google.com/us/think/measurement/incrementality-testing/); [Google Ads Help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/14102986?hl=en)). Marketing-mix modeling (MMM) can estimate channel contribution over time using aggregated data, but Nielsen notes that model quality depends on granular inputs, business context, validation, and periodic refreshes ([Nielsen](https://www.nielsen.com/insights/2014/marketing-mix-modeling-what-marketers-need-to-know/)).

### Core metric definitions

\[
\text{Paid CAC} = \frac{\text{paid media + creative + directly attributable fees}}{\text{new customers attributed to paid acquisition}}
\]

\[
\text{Contribution per order} = \text{net revenue} - \text{variable product, fulfillment, payment, discount, return, and channel costs}
\]

\[
\text{Contribution LTV} = \sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\text{expected contribution in period }t}{(1+r)^t}
\]

\[
\text{CAC payback} = \text{time until cumulative customer contribution covers acquisition cost}
\]

\[
\text{MER} = \frac{\text{total net revenue}}{\text{total marketing spend}}
\]

Shopify distinguishes CAC from related metrics and defines MER as a blended view of total revenue relative to marketing spend; both are useful only when interpreted with margin, new-customer mix, and incrementality ([Shopify](https://www.shopify.com/blog/customer-acquisition-cost); [Shopify](https://www.shopify.com/blog/marketing-efficiency-ratio)).

### Measurement ladder

1. **Operational truth:** reconcile orders, cancellations, discounts, taxes, channel fees, returns, and COGS.
2. **Channel reporting:** standard UTMs, promo codes, call tracking where appropriate, marketplace data, and post-purchase survey.
3. **Cohorts:** acquisition month × channel × first product/occasion; track repeat, returns, contribution, and referral.
4. **Experiments:** holdout audiences, matched geographies, paused regions, or randomized offers.
5. **MMM:** consider when spend, time series, and channel complexity are sufficient; do not force it onto tiny datasets.
6. **Decision cadence:** weekly optimization, monthly cohort review, quarterly budget reallocation.

### Archetype-specific scorecards

| Layer | Pizza shop | Yoga-mat brand |
|---|---|---|
| Demand | Maps impressions, nonbrand search, delivery-app discovery | Nonbrand search, marketplace impressions, creator reach |
| Conversion | Calls, order completion, average order value | Product-page conversion, cart completion, marketplace conversion |
| Quality | Accuracy, delivery time, refunds, review themes | Returns, defect rate, review themes, support contacts |
| Economics | Contribution/order, new-customer CAC, 30/60/90-day repeat | Contribution/order, CAC, payback, cohort LTV, return-adjusted ROAS |
| Brand | Direct orders, branded search, local recognition | Branded search, direct traffic, asset recognition, new-customer penetration |
| Causal | Matched-neighborhood lift, offer holdouts | Geo/creator holdouts, conversion lift, marketplace incrementality |

Avoid optimizing to followers, impressions, clicks, email-list size, platform ROAS, or NPS alone. These may diagnose a mechanism; none proves profitable growth.

## 14. Common mistakes and anti-patterns

### 1. Inventing a category for a familiar product

New terminology adds cognitive work without creating demand. Use the known category, then clarify the use case and proof. Dunford’s framework treats market category as the context that makes differentiated value obvious, not as a creativity contest ([April Dunford](https://www.aprildunford.com/post/a-quickstart-guide-to-positioning)).

### 2. Claiming “quality” without making it visible

“Best,” “premium,” “authentic,” and “sustainable” are weak when unproved. Replace adjectives with ingredients, process, specifications, demonstrations, verified reviews, guarantees, or service standards.

### 3. Copying category aesthetics

Mimicking the leading competitor may feel safe but increases misattribution. Build and measure distinctive assets, then preserve them ([Ehrenberg-Bass Institute](https://marketingscience.info/news-and-insights/brands-of-distinction)).

### 4. Treating loyalty as the only growth engine

Heavy users are valuable, but a small brand cannot retain customers it never acquires. The double-jeopardy pattern makes penetration a continuing requirement ([Ehrenberg-Bass Institute](https://marketingscience.info/news-and-insights/effective-brand-growth-acquisition-or-retention)).

### 5. Over-discounting

Continuous discounts train deal-seeking, compress contribution, and obscure whether positioning works. Use offers to create a specific behavior—trial, larger basket, off-peak demand, referral, or reactivation—and measure incrementality.

### 6. Chasing every channel

Thin budgets produce no learning. Match channel to job, test with explicit thresholds, and concentrate on one demand-capture and one demand-creation engine before expanding.

### 7. Confusing attributed revenue with incremental revenue

Retargeting, branded search, delivery apps, and affiliates often touch customers who may have purchased anyway. Use holdouts and blended economics; Google’s incrementality framework exists precisely to estimate causality rather than correlation ([Google Think](https://business.google.com/en-all/think/measurement/marketing-experimentation-incrementality-testing/)).

### 8. Ignoring the rented-shelf tax

Marketplaces and aggregators offer high-intent distribution but charge fees and control customer access. Measure their incremental contribution, build direct permission where allowed, and never assume gross order value equals owned-channel revenue.

### 9. Letting review collection become a campaign

Review recency and volume decay. Make requests and responses part of the fulfillment workflow; BrightLocal’s evidence shows that customers inspect freshness, consistency, and owner response as well as the average star rating ([BrightLocal](https://www.brightlocal.com/research/local-consumer-review-survey/)).

### 10. Scaling before fixing conversion and retention

Higher spend cannot rescue a slow site, out-of-stock item, confusing menu, weak listing, high return rate, or unreliable fulfillment. It simply purchases more expensive failure.

### 11. Using one creative everywhere

The brand assets should remain consistent, but the proof and format should match the CEP and channel. A Maps ad captures “now”; a creator video demonstrates; an email triggers repeat; packaging reinforces memory.

### 12. Applying benchmark ratios as laws

The 60/40 split, 5% retention claim, LTV:CAC conventions, and cross-industry conversion benchmarks are starting points. Use the business’s own margins, frequency, cohorts, and experiments to set the budget.

## 15. A gated 90-day marketing plan

The plan is deliberately gated: later spend depends on earlier evidence. Each week produces a decision asset, not just activity.

### Phase 1: Truth, economics, and positioning — Weeks 1–3

#### Week 1 — Baseline the commercial journey

**General**

- Reconcile 90–180 days of orders, customers, marketing spend, discounts, refunds/returns, variable costs, and channel fees.
- Calculate contribution by order/SKU and source; separate new and returning customers.
- Establish rating, review velocity, repeat, return/refund, and complaint baselines.
- Interview 8–12 recent buyers, several lapsed customers, and several dissatisfied customers about occasion, alternatives, decision, proof, and experience.

**Pizza illustration:** tag orders by pickup, direct delivery, app, dine-in, daypart, radius, and basket; measure median promised versus actual time and identify high-margin occasions.

**Yoga-mat illustration:** tag cohorts by SKU, channel, discount, creator, return reason, and first stated use case; inspect review language for repeated grip, smell, weight, cushioning, and durability themes.

**Gate:** Reliable unit contribution and a ranked list of experience issues exist. If not, do not increase acquisition.

#### Week 2 — Map CEPs and choose the beachhead

**General**

- Build a CEP map from interviews, search queries, reviews, customer-service logs, and sales timing.
- Score each segment/occasion on demand, margin, reachability, competitive intensity, proof fit, and referral density.
- Choose one primary and one secondary CEP; define exclusion conditions.
- Write the competitive-alternative list and proof hierarchy.

**Pizza:** primary CEP might be Friday family dinner within a 12-minute delivery radius; secondary might be office lunch catering.

**Yoga mat:** primary CEP might be beginner hot-yoga students; secondary might be frequent travelers seeking a lighter mat.

**Gate:** The team can state buyer, occasion, alternative, promise, and proof in one sentence.

#### Week 3 — Build positioning and distinctive assets

**General**

- Draft three positioning territories; test comprehension and preference with target buyers.
- Audit competitor colors, phrases, packaging, thumbnails, and claims.
- Select the minimum asset system and create production templates.
- Define claim rules and evidence required.

**Pizza:** redesign menu, box sticker, Google images, and social templates around one recognizable palette and the crisp/on-time proof.

**Yoga mat:** align product-page hero, packaging, mat mark, creator frame, and email design around one recognizable cue and the stability/easy-care proof.

**Gate:** Buyers accurately retell the offer and attribute unbranded assets more often than chance.

### Phase 2: Availability and proof — Weeks 4–6

#### Week 4 — Repair the digital shelf

**General**

- Fix mobile speed, navigation, analytics, checkout/order path, core metadata, and conversion tracking.
- Standardize business/product information across major surfaces.
- Create one landing page for each chosen CEP.

**Pizza:** complete Google Business Profile, hours, menu, categories, order links, photography, delivery area, location schema, and catering page.

**Yoga mat:** rebuild the DTC product page; improve marketplace titles, bullets, images, attributes, backend terms, A+ Content if eligible, shipping, and returns.

**Gate:** A buyer can discover, evaluate, and purchase in a short mobile session with no contradictory information.

#### Week 5 — Install the review and UGC engine

**General**

- Automate compliant post-purchase review requests.
- Create response standards and issue-routing tags.
- Ask selected customers for permissioned photo/video proof.
- Build a proof library indexed by CEP, objection, and channel.

**Pizza:** request reviews after fulfilled orders; add a QR prompt to receipts or packaging; film delivery-condition and behind-the-scenes proof.

**Yoga mat:** request a verified review after multiple uses; invite a structured grip/cleaning demonstration; tag footage by practice type.

**Gate:** Review requests fire reliably, response ownership is clear, and at least ten reusable proof assets are available.

#### Week 6 — Launch the organic decision-content base

**General**

- Publish 3–5 high-intent pages and 6–10 short-form assets tied to CEPs.
- Establish two recurring social series and a repurposing workflow.
- Link content to the relevant purchase path and capture first-party permission.

**Pizza:** publish catering quantities, delivery-area, reheating, and family-order pages; start “Friday oven exit” and neighborhood-partner series.

**Yoga mat:** publish thickness, cleaning, hot-yoga, beginner, and comparison pages; start grip-test and instructor-tip series.

**Gate:** Content is indexed or distributed, uses distinctive assets, and each item has a measurable commercial job.

### Phase 3: Controlled acquisition and borrowed distribution — Weeks 7–10

#### Week 7 — Launch high-intent paid capture

**General**

- Set up brand/nonbrand separation, exclusions, geo rules, conversion values, and new-customer reporting.
- Launch a small number of tightly themed campaigns.
- Use contribution-based CAC ceilings.

**Pizza:** Search/Maps for delivery, pickup, and catering in the true service radius; keep delivery-app economics separate.

**Yoga mat:** Shopping/Search and marketplace campaigns for high-intent use-case terms; protect brand terms without letting them mask nonbrand performance.

**Gate:** Tracking reconciles reasonably to order systems, and early contribution is inside the predeclared test ceiling.

#### Week 8 — Test paid demand creation

**General**

- Launch 3–5 genuinely different creative concepts, each preserving brand assets.
- Test one variable at a time where practical: CEP, proof, format, or offer.
- Exclude existing customers from new-customer tests and report retargeting separately.

**Pizza:** compare family-relief, craftsmanship, and local-social-proof concepts within the service radius.

**Yoga mat:** compare grip demonstration, beginner confidence, and cleaning-ease concepts using Meta or TikTok.

**Gate:** At least one concept produces qualified new customers with plausible payback; otherwise revise message or proof before raising budget.

#### Week 9 — Launch partnership pilots

**General**

- Recruit five tightly matched partners.
- Provide the brand/proof kit, tracking mechanism, economics, and disclosure terms.
- Design one repeated collaboration rather than many logo swaps.

**Pizza:** pilot office lunch, brewery/event, apartment, school, or micro-creator partnerships.

**Yoga mat:** pilot two instructors, two studios, and one complementary brand or specialist creator.

**Gate:** Partner activity creates attributable new-customer behavior and a credible incrementality test.

#### Week 10 — Optimize conversion, not just traffic

**General**

- Review search terms, creative comments, session behavior, checkout abandonment, calls, and returns.
- Improve the highest-friction step.
- Reallocate budget from losing audience-message-channel cells to winners.
- Run an offer holdout or geo holdout where feasible.

**Pizza:** test order-page clarity, pickup instructions, family bundle framing, and delivery promise—not blanket discounts.

**Yoga mat:** test hero proof, comparison chart, review placement, shipping/return clarity, and full-price versus value-added bundle.

**Gate:** Conversion improves without a deterioration in margin, return/refund rate, or customer quality.

### Phase 4: Retention, incrementality, and budget decision — Weeks 11–13

#### Week 11 — Install lifecycle and referral loops

**General**

- Build welcome/post-purchase, service-recovery, repeat/replenishment, reactivation, and referral flows.
- Segment by observed occasion and behavior.
- Cap frequency and hold out a control group if the platform permits.

**Pizza:** saved-favorite reorder, occasion reminders, lapsed-customer message, and a two-sided referral test.

**Yoga mat:** setup/care sequence, class-use education, accessory cross-sell, review request, and instructor/referral flow.

**Gate:** Messages are triggered by customer value, not a generic broadcast calendar; contribution is measurable.

#### Week 12 — Measure incrementality and brand signals

**General**

- Compare exposed versus holdout geographies, audiences, or customer cells.
- Review branded search, direct traffic/orders, new-customer share, asset recognition, review velocity, and CEP-linked survey response.
- Recalculate CAC, payback, MER, and expected contribution LTV using actual early cohorts.

**Pizza:** compare matched neighborhoods or delivery zones and inspect direct-order migration without assuming it is incremental.

**Yoga mat:** compare creator/geo cells, marketplace versus DTC overlap, and return-adjusted cohort value.

**Gate:** At least one acquisition mechanism shows evidence of incremental contribution, not just attributed revenue.

#### Week 13 — Make the next-quarter portfolio decision

Classify each initiative:

- **Scale:** incremental contribution is positive, payback acceptable, and fulfillment quality holds.
- **Improve:** demand exists but conversion, creative, proof, or economics have a clear fix.
- **Maintain:** brand or organic mechanism is compounding but needs more time.
- **Stop:** no credible response after a fair test, or economics fail at the margin.

Set the next-quarter budget across:

1. demand capture;
2. demand creation/brand;
3. retention and referral;
4. experimentation and measurement.

Do not allocate by habit. Fund the combination that expands availability and penetration while preserving customer experience and contribution.

## Final operating principles

1. **Use the category; do not fight it.** Existing demand is an asset.
2. **Make quality legible.** Specific proof beats generic superiority.
3. **Be recognizable before being elaborate.** Repeat a small set of distinctive assets.
4. **Own occasions, then broaden.** CEPs connect memory to real purchase moments.
5. **Treat every shelf as media.** Maps, storefronts, marketplaces, retail, packaging, and checkout all market.
6. **Assume auction pressure.** Better creative, conversion, retention, and organic demand protect economics.
7. **Build reach and retention together.** Penetration drives scale; profitable repeat improves allowable CAC.
8. **Keep reviews fresh and operational.** Reputation is a continuous system.
9. **Borrow trust carefully.** Partners must contribute relevant reach and measurable customer quality.
10. **Separate activation from brand, then reunite them in the budget.** Near-term harvesting without future demand eventually stalls.
11. **Measure marginal, incremental contribution.** Attribution is evidence, not causality.
12. **Scale only after gates are passed.** In a commodity market, disciplined execution is itself the advantage.
