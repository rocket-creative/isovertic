# The B2B SaaS Product-Led Growth Marketing Playbook

## Executive summary

Product-led growth (PLG) is not “offer a free plan and wait.” It is a go-to-market system in which the product performs work traditionally assigned to marketing and sales: it demonstrates value, produces shareable artifacts, recruits collaborators, captures behavioral intent, and creates expansion signals. Marketing’s job is therefore to **engineer and amplify a compounding system**, not merely fill a lead funnel. The practical hierarchy is activation before acquisition, retention before scale, and product-qualified behavior before form-fill intent.

The historical evidence explains the enthusiasm, but it should not be read as a timeless causal guarantee. OpenView’s 2022 benchmark found PLG leaders growing 50% year over year versus 21% for traditional SaaS, with 128% net retention; an earlier public-company analysis found PLG companies generating 1.7 times more gross profit per dollar spent on sales and product ([OpenView 2022 SaaS Benchmarks](https://offers.openviewpartners.com/hubfs/2022%20SaaS%20Benchmarks/OpenView%20SaaS%20Benchmarks%20Report_2022.pdf); [OpenView financial and operating benchmarks](https://openviewpartners.com/blog/finance-operating-benchmarks/)). Public-market multiples in OpenView’s PLG Index also traded at a substantial premium during the 2020–2021 market, but multiples later compressed; treat the index as evidence that capital markets once rewarded PLG economics, not proof that declaring a company “product-led” creates value ([Growth Unhinged on the PLG Index](https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/look-at-this-metric-to-see-whether); [OpenView on pricing transformations](https://openviewpartners.com/blog/pricing-transformations-2021/)).

The strongest PLG marketing program has six interlocking engines:

1. **Intent capture:** jobs-to-be-done positioning, comparison pages, use-case content, and high-intent SEO.
2. **Value delivery:** a short path from landing page to a completed, meaningful outcome.
3. **Product distribution:** invitations, shared outputs, integrations, embeds, attribution, and network effects.
4. **Education and supply:** templates, creators, community experts, courses, and certifications that help users succeed.
5. **Behavioral monetization:** packaging and lifecycle messages that ask for payment after value, plus PQL/PQA routing when human help raises conversion or account penetration.
6. **Measurement:** acquisition-to-retention cohorts, north-star inputs, causal paid-media tests, and unit economics by source and segment.

This playbook concerns PLG marketing. Enterprise procurement, account planning, security reviews, executive alignment, and complex negotiation belong in the companion sales-led playbook. They enter here only at the boundary where demonstrated product usage merits sales assist.

## 1. What makes PLG marketing different

### The product is the proof, channel, and intent sensor

In a sales-led motion, marketing makes a promise and sales interprets it. In PLG, the product must let a user verify the promise. The website should route qualified visitors into use, not into a calendar by default. The product then becomes:

- **Proof:** users experience the workflow rather than watch a generic demo.
- **Media:** shared files, recordings, dashboards, links, embeds, and invitations expose non-users.
- **Merchandising:** limits, upgrade moments, and contextual prompts explain paid value.
- **Research:** search terms, setup choices, feature use, and collaboration reveal jobs and objections.
- **Qualification:** repeated or team-level use is stronger intent than downloading an ebook.

OpenView’s PLG principles capture this operating model: build for the end user and discovery; meet users where they work; support openness and flexibility; deliver instant product value and customer experience; build community; and monetize after value, often in relation to usage ([OpenView 2022 SaaS Benchmarks](https://offers.openviewpartners.com/hubfs/2022%20SaaS%20Benchmarks/OpenView%20SaaS%20Benchmarks%20Report_2022.pdf)). Wes Bush’s formulation similarly treats PLG as a company-wide strategy in which the product drives acquisition, conversion, and expansion—not a marketing campaign attached to a conventional motion ([ProductLed’s PLG book](https://productled.com/book/product-led-growth)).

### Activation is the first marketing KPI

Acquisition magnifies whatever happens after signup. If only a small share of signups experiences value, doubling traffic doubles waste, support burden, and retargeting audiences—not durable growth. OpenView found that standout PLG companies commonly define and track activation, while its benchmarks frame “instant value” as a core PLG principle ([OpenView’s 2022 benchmark analysis](https://openviewpartners.com/blog/how-do-you-stack-up-2022-saas-benchmarks/)). Amplitude’s cross-product research makes the urgency concrete: day-seven behavior strongly predicts longer-term retention, and 69% of top day-seven performers were also top performers at three months ([Amplitude’s 7% retention rule](https://amplitude.com/blog/7-percent-retention-rule)).

The marketing implication is organizational: lifecycle, content, brand, performance, and product marketing should share an activation target with product growth. Campaign briefs must specify not only audience, message, and signup CPA, but also the **target job**, **activation event**, **time window**, and **expected retained behavior**. The best campaign is not the one with the cheapest account; it is the one producing the lowest cost per retained, monetizable account.

### Time-to-value is the conversion surface

Time-to-value (TTV) is the elapsed time from a user’s initial intent to a meaningful outcome. It includes pre-signup friction, authentication, setup, data import, integrations, learning, and the first successful workflow. ProductLed argues that speed alone is incomplete: “activation velocity” should combine how quickly and how often users complete value-bearing actions ([ProductLed on activation velocity](https://productled.com/blog/activation-velocity)). A collaboration product can shorten TTV with a sample workspace, but durable activation may require the user to invite a teammate and repeat the workflow.

Manage TTV as a distribution:

- Median time to first value.
- 75th/90th percentile TTV, exposing users stuck in setup.
- Percentage achieving value in session one, day one, and week one.
- TTV by intent page, source, persona, template, device, and acquisition campaign.
- Time from individual value to collaborative value.

### PQLs replace—not merely supplement—MQL logic

An MQL is usually inferred from marketing behavior: title, firmographics, content downloads, email engagement, or event attendance. A product-qualified lead (PQL) is inferred from actual product behavior after firsthand use. OpenView’s definition emphasizes bottom-of-funnel product interactions rather than middle-of-funnel marketing interactions ([OpenView’s PQL guide](https://openviewpartners.com/blog/how-to-identify-a-product-qualified-lead)). ProductLed’s practical models combine ideal-customer fit, usage intensity, and buying signals rather than declaring every signup sales-ready ([ProductLed on defining PQLs](https://productled.com/blog/how-to-define-pqls)).

For B2B products, the account is often the more useful unit. A **product-qualified account (PQA)** may contain an activated champion, several collaborators, usage growth, an integration, and a threshold event. A salesperson can then help with workspace consolidation, governance, procurement, or an enterprise feature. Marketing owns the signal design, education, routing, and message; sales owns the human intervention. Do not route low-context “PQLs” merely because they logged in three times.

## 2. Step 1 — Position for self-serve buyers

### Start with a narrow job, not a broad category

Self-serve buyers cannot rely on a salesperson to translate vague positioning. The homepage and intent pages must answer: “Can this do the job I need, for someone like me, with my tools, under my constraints?” Use jobs-to-be-done interviews to map:

1. **Trigger:** what changed?
2. **Struggling moment:** what is expensive, slow, risky, or embarrassing?
3. **Desired progress:** what outcome defines success?
4. **Current workaround:** spreadsheet, internal tool, incumbent, manual process, or no action.
5. **Anxieties:** setup, migration, security, lock-in, quality, team adoption.
6. **Habit and allegiance:** why the workaround persists.

Translate this into an intent architecture: `[job] software`, `[job] template`, `[competitor] alternative`, `[product] vs [competitor]`, `[integration A] + [integration B]`, and role/industry pages only where the workflow and proof genuinely differ. The product starting state should match the page promise. A visitor arriving on “user research repository template” should enter a populated research repository—not a blank general-purpose workspace.

### Build a message ladder for autonomous evaluation

Every high-intent page needs five layers:

- **Outcome:** the progress the user wants.
- **Mechanism:** how the product creates it.
- **Proof:** a product view, interactive example, user result, or credible benchmark.
- **Friction answer:** setup time, compatibility, migration, permissions, and pricing.
- **Action:** start from a relevant template, try a focused utility, or create the first artifact.

The consumerization of enterprise software means users expect immediate access, understandable packaging, polished UX, and proof before talking to sales, consistent with [a16z’s account of software’s expanding audiences](https://a16z.com/the-great-expansion-a-new-era-of-consumer-software/). Gartner describes B2B buying as a nonlinear set of buying jobs and argues for digital experiences that support independent learning while preserving seller assistance for harder tasks ([Gartner on the B2B buying journey](https://www.gartner.com/en/sales/insights/b2b-buying-journey); [Gartner on seller-assisted digital buying](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/gartner-says-b2b-sales-organizations-need-to-give-customers-a-se)). HubSpot’s buyer research likewise finds that channel preference depends on deal context, reinforcing that self-service and human help should be options rather than ideological absolutes ([HubSpot buyer interaction research](https://blog.hubspot.com/sales/person-phone-or-online-sales)).

### Positioning deliverables

Within 30 days, produce:

- A job map with trigger, desired outcome, alternatives, and anxieties.
- A category and “why now” narrative, but no category jargon without a concrete outcome.
- A message matrix by primary job and user, including proof and objection.
- A search-intent map linked to landing page, template/tool, and activation event.
- An alternative/comparison policy grounded in honest fit—not feature-table theater.
- A five-minute unmoderated test: can a target user explain who the product is for, what it does, why it is different, what it costs, and how to try it?

## 3. Step 2 — Design acquisition loops, not a pile of funnels

### Funnel accounting versus loop mechanics

A funnel describes conversion across stages; it remains useful for diagnosis. A loop describes how the output of one cycle creates input for the next. Reforge’s canonical distinction is that loops compound while funnels require fresh input; the question is not “What channel did the user come from?” but “What user action produces the next user, content object, or distribution event?” ([Reforge, “Growth Loops Are the New Funnels”](https://www.reforge.com/blog/growth-loops)). Casey Winters similarly frames sustainable growth as a system that must evolve as one S-curve saturates and the company sequences the next product or loop ([Casey Winters on S-curves and product sequencing](https://caseyaccidental.com/s-curves-and-product-sequencing/)).

Keep both views:

| View | Best question | Primary use |
|---|---|---|
| Funnel | Where do users drop? | Conversion diagnosis |
| Loop | What output creates the next input? | Compounding growth design |
| Cohort | Which users retain and monetize? | Quality and unit economics |
| Network | Does value rise with relevant participation? | Defensibility and cold-start diagnosis |

### Four PLG loop archetypes

**1. Collaborative loop.** A user creates a workspace or object, invites colleagues, collaborators experience value, and some create new workspaces. Slack-style team communication, Figma files, Notion workspaces, and Linear issues fit this pattern. Optimize invite relevance, recipient landing pages, permission clarity, and the recipient’s first action—not raw invite volume.

**2. Content/output loop.** A user creates a public or externally shared output carrying product attribution: a Loom video, Cal.com scheduling link, Canva design, form, report, status page, or embedded chart. Recipients consume value before signup. Measure exposed recipients, attributed visits, recipient activation, and creator retention. Never trade user trust for aggressive watermarks.

**3. Incentivized loop.** Both sides receive storage, credits, usage, or a premium capability after a qualified referral. Reward activated or retained referrals, not email submissions; add fraud controls and a cap. Incentives should accelerate a naturally valuable behavior rather than compensate for weak value.

**4. Content/data/network loop.** More creators, integrations, templates, plugins, public examples, or user-generated data improve discovery and product utility; better utility attracts more contributors. Andrew Chen’s network-effect framework stresses density, activity, quality, and relevance—not just registered-user count—and warns that a network without enough relevant participants faces a cold-start problem ([Andrew Chen, *The Cold Start Problem*](https://andrewchen.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/ColdStartProb_9780062969743_AS0928_cc20_Final.pdf); [chapter preview](https://andrewchen.com/chapter-one-cold-start/)).

### Write each loop as an equation

For an invitation loop:

\[
\text{Loop multiplier} =
\text{invites per activated user}
\times \text{invite acceptance}
\times \text{recipient activation}
\times \text{recipient reproduction}
\]

Also measure loop cycle time. A multiplier below one can still make a channel valuable, but it is not self-sustaining. Segment by team size and job: a design-review workflow may naturally invite more users than a private analytics workflow. Growth Unhinged’s virality guide distinguishes exposure from true product virality and emphasizes that not every product should force an invitation mechanic ([Growth Unhinged on product virality](https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/your-guide-to-product-virality)). OpenView’s product-virality examples similarly focus on building sharing and collaboration into core value rather than attaching a generic referral widget ([OpenView on viral product design](https://openviewpartners.com/blog/how-to-build-your-product-for-viral-growth/)).

### Loop review checklist

- Is the loop action necessary for the user’s job?
- Does the recipient get value before being asked to sign up?
- Is attribution visible but respectful?
- What is the loop multiplier and cycle time?
- Which step is the constraint?
- Does the loop attract the right next user?
- Does it improve or harm retention and trust?
- Can spam, abuse, or low-quality supply poison the loop?

## 4. Step 3 — Make SEO a product surface

### Why SEO fits PLG

PLG captures demand when users are already trying to solve a job. Search can route them directly into a utility, template, integration, or product workflow; the landing asset delivers value and generates intent data. The strategic advantage is not “publish many blog posts.” It is a connected library of pages that are individually useful, collectively authoritative, and operationally linked to activation.

OpenView’s product benchmarks identify discoverability and meeting users where they work as PLG principles ([OpenView 2022 SaaS Benchmarks](https://offers.openviewpartners.com/hubfs/2022%20SaaS%20Benchmarks/OpenView%20SaaS%20Benchmarks%20Report_2022.pdf)). HubSpot’s long-running Website Grader demonstrates the free-utility pattern: by 2009, HubSpot reported that the tool had analyzed more than two million URLs, turning a diagnostic job into discovery and lead capture ([HubSpot on Website Grader](https://www.hubspot.com/blog/bid/5539/website-grader-analyzes-over-2-million-sites)). Ahrefs uses the same principle with free website and SEO checkers that expose a useful slice of the paid product ([Ahrefs Website Checker](https://ahrefs.com/website-checker); [Ahrefs SEO Checker](https://ahrefs.com/seo-checker)).

### Build the SEO portfolio in layers

1. **High-intent commercial:** category, use case, role, industry, comparison, alternative, and pricing pages.
2. **Workflow:** how to complete the job, including examples and an executable next step.
3. **Programmatic:** integrations, templates, public objects, glossaries, directories, calculators, or benchmark pages generated from structured data.
4. **Free utility:** audit, converter, checker, generator, calculator, preview, or sandbox.
5. **Education:** original research, expert guidance, and curricula that create category authority.

Zapier’s programmatic SEO guidance is appropriately cautious: use a structured template where search demand and data vary meaningfully, preserve unique value on each page, and avoid publishing thin pages merely because they can be generated ([Zapier’s programmatic SEO guide](https://zapier.com/blog/programmatic-seo/)). Its app directory is powerful because each pair of apps represents an actual integration job, and the page can lead directly into creating an automation. Canva paired scalable landing pages with educational content and reported growing Design School traffic to one million visits, illustrating how product-adjacent education and templates reinforce one another ([Canva’s content strategy](https://www.canva.com/learn/content-marketing-strategy/); [Foundation’s Canva SEO analysis](https://foundationinc.co/lab/canva-seo)).

### Programmatic SEO acceptance criteria

Do not launch a page type until it passes:

- **Demand:** the intent exists or is predictably emerging.
- **Unique data:** each page has genuinely distinct inputs, examples, compatibility, or outputs.
- **Utility:** a visitor can decide or do something.
- **Product bridge:** the CTA opens the exact workflow.
- **Index quality:** canonicalization, pagination, faceting, and low-value combinations are controlled.
- **Supply maintenance:** data owners, refresh frequency, and deprecation rules are defined.
- **Conversion instrumentation:** page → utility/template use → activation → retained account is measurable.

Traffic estimates are directional, not truth. Ahrefs explicitly studies error in third-party traffic estimation; Search Console, product analytics, and cohort revenue should arbitrate decisions ([Ahrefs on traffic-estimation accuracy](https://ahrefs.com/blog/traffic-estimations-accuracy/)). Semrush’s organic-distribution documentation is useful for competitive discovery, but it too should seed hypotheses rather than substitute for first-party outcomes ([Semrush organic search dashboard](https://www.semrush.com/kb/1515-traffic-and-market-traffic-distribution-organic-search)).

### SEO operating model

Create cross-functional “page-type” teams: product marketing owns intent and message; SEO owns demand, information architecture, and indexation; product/design owns utility; engineering owns structured generation; analytics owns activation and revenue measurement. Review page types, not isolated posts. The dashboard should show indexed pages, non-brand clicks, qualified visits, workflow starts, activation, retained workspaces, assisted invitations, and payback by landing-page cohort.

## 5. Step 4 — Use free tools, templates, and utilities as the top of funnel

### The asset must finish a small job

A conventional lead magnet delays value: submit information, receive content, then interpret it. A PLG asset performs a bounded job now. A checker diagnoses, a calculator quantifies, a converter transforms, a generator produces, and a template removes setup. That makes the asset simultaneously a search destination, proof of competence, retargeting seed, feedback mechanism, and path into the product.

Use a simple selection rubric:

| Criterion | Question |
|---|---|
| Job proximity | Is the utility adjacent to the paid product’s recurring job? |
| Standalone value | Does the user get a credible result without sales contact? |
| Natural continuation | Is saving, repeating, sharing, or operationalizing the output better in-product? |
| Search/share potential | Do users actively seek or pass around this result? |
| Data advantage | Does usage reveal intent or improve the utility without violating trust? |
| Maintenance | Can the company keep logic, content, security, and outputs accurate? |

HubSpot’s Website Grader and successor Marketing Grader used a free assessment to show expertise and create a logical bridge to marketing software ([HubSpot’s Marketing Grader launch](https://blog.hubspot.com/blog/tabid/6307/bid/29274/hubspot-launches-free-marketing-grader-tool-to-replace-website-grader.aspx)). Ahrefs’ checkers reveal real SEO issues and then make ongoing research, monitoring, and remediation easier in the main product ([Ahrefs Website Checker](https://ahrefs.com/website-checker)). The best free tool is a “single-player mode” of the recurring paid job—not an unrelated traffic stunt.

### Templates are product distribution

Templates encode expert practice, reduce empty-state anxiety, target long-tail search intent, and let creators bring audiences. Notion describes its gallery as a home for creators and builders, with discovery and contribution at the center ([Notion’s reimagined Template Gallery](https://www.notion.com/blog/new-notion-template-gallery); [Notion’s gallery announcement](https://www.notion.com/blog/new-template-gallery)). Figma Community combines files, plugins, and other reusable resources, making community supply part of the product experience ([Figma Community](https://www.figma.com/community)). Canva’s template-rich model connects a search query to an editable starting point rather than a static article, while its education creates demand and competence around the workflow ([Canva Design School strategy](https://www.canva.com/learn/content-marketing-strategy/)).

Build the template portfolio as a marketplace:

- Prioritize templates by job frequency, activation lift, search demand, and strategic segment.
- Publish first-party “gold standard” templates before opening supply.
- Require preview, expected outcome, setup steps, author, updated date, and compatibility.
- Rank by successful use and retained outcomes, not clicks alone.
- Let users remix and attribute creators.
- Establish review, abuse, and deprecation processes.
- Invite domain experts whose audiences overlap the ideal user.

For Zapier, the analogous asset is the integration recipe: “connect tool A and tool B” becomes a highly specific page and an executable automation. Its programmatic SEO guidance reinforces that scalable pages need unique utility and a clear user journey, not keyword substitution ([Zapier on programmatic SEO](https://zapier.com/blog/programmatic-seo/)).

### Gate progressively, not reflexively

Choose the minimum identity needed for the job:

- No gate for discovery, preview, or one-time output when reach matters.
- Lightweight account to save, revisit, personalize, or share securely.
- Workspace creation when collaboration is the value.
- Payment when recurring usage, volume, governance, or premium capability justifies it.
- Sales contact only when implementation or organizational complexity makes help valuable.

Measure tool/template visitors through retained value: start rate, completion, result quality, save/share rate, account creation, activation, repeat use, invitations, paid conversion, and assisted revenue. A tool with massive traffic and no recurring job adjacency can be more expensive than it looks.

## 6. Step 5 — Engineer onboarding and activation

### Define the aha moment as evidence, not folklore

The “aha moment” is the earliest behavior or state indicating the user has experienced the product’s core value. It is not the first login, a tour completion, or an arbitrary feature click. Define candidate events from qualitative research, then validate which event—or sequence—predicts retention and monetization after controlling for segment and source.

Use this process:

1. Interview newly retained, newly churned, and stuck users about intended progress.
2. Identify candidate value events and prerequisite events.
3. Compare retention for users who do and do not complete them in a fixed window.
4. Test frequency, breadth, collaboration, and sequence—not just binary completion.
5. Choose one comprehensible activation definition per major use case.
6. Revalidate after product, ICP, pricing, or acquisition mix changes.

Lenny Rachitsky’s activation guide emphasizes identifying the action most correlated with retention and making the metric practical enough to guide teams ([Lenny’s guide to determining activation](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-to-determine-your-activation)). ProductLed recommends selecting user-activation metrics tied to realized value rather than vanity engagement ([ProductLed on activation metrics](https://productled.com/blog/user-activation-metrics)). Amplitude’s research shows why the first week deserves special attention: seven-day activation was the strongest early predictor of three-month retention in its dataset ([Amplitude’s retention benchmark](https://amplitude.com/blog/7-percent-retention-rule)).

### Design the shortest truthful path to value

Map onboarding as a critical path and remove every step that does not support the first job:

- Pre-fill defaults from the acquisition page, role, or chosen template.
- Defer profile enrichment, team configuration, and optional integrations.
- Offer sample data, but distinguish the simulated outcome from the user’s real outcome.
- Import from the incumbent and preserve structure.
- Ask one intent question only when it meaningfully changes the path.
- Use checklists for a small number of outcome steps, not feature tours.
- Celebrate completed work, not interface exploration.
- Trigger collaboration after the user has something worth sharing.

The empty state is a marketing surface. It should restate the job, show a credible finished example, and offer the fewest paths to a useful first artifact. Appcues’ product-led onboarding framework similarly recommends contextual guidance aligned with the user’s journey rather than a generic tour ([Appcues on product-led onboarding](https://www.appcues.com/blog/product-led-onboarding)). Pendo recommends measuring completion, time to complete, feature adoption, user sentiment, and downstream behavior to judge onboarding effectiveness ([Pendo on measuring onboarding](https://www.pendo.io/resources/measuring-onboarding-effectiveness/)).

### Orchestrate in-product and lifecycle messaging

Tools such as Pendo, Appcues, and Intercom can accelerate experimentation, but messaging cannot rescue a fundamentally long workflow. Use:

- **Contextual prompts** when a user encounters a relevant empty state or prerequisite.
- **Behavior-triggered email** when the next step is easier outside the product or the session ended.
- **Human offer** when a high-fit account stalls on a complex configuration.
- **Milestone messages** when users complete meaningful value and can advance to collaboration or repeat use.
- **Limit messages** that explain the paid outcome and current usage, not vague “upgrade now” pressure.

Intercom’s onboarding guidance recommends a sequenced campaign with explicit goals and behavior-based audiences rather than sending every new user the same schedule ([Intercom onboarding series guide](https://www.intercom.com/help/en/articles/421-create-an-effective-onboarding-series); [Intercom’s onboarding message schedule](https://www.intercom.com/blog/designing-onboarding-message-schedule/)). Suppress messages after task completion. Hold out a percentage of users so the team learns whether messaging causes incremental activation.

### Instrument the activation model

Amplitude, Mixpanel, or Heap should capture:

- Signup source, landing page, campaign, template/tool, and declared job.
- Critical-path events with consistent properties.
- Time between steps and error states.
- Collaboration and share events.
- Account-level identity stitching.
- Exposure to experiments and lifecycle messages.
- Activation version, so definition changes do not rewrite history.

Amplitude describes product analytics as the behavioral analysis of journeys, cohorts, engagement, and retention; Heap emphasizes observing adoption and identifying friction in actual behavior ([Amplitude’s product analytics guide](https://amplitude.com/explore/analytics/product-analytics-guide); [Heap on product adoption](https://www.heap.io/use-case/product-adoption)). Mixpanel’s product-metrics guide places acquisition, activation, engagement, retention, and monetization in one measurement system ([Mixpanel’s product metrics guide](https://mixpanel.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/eBook_Guide-to-Product-Metrics.pdf)).

### Choose a north-star metric and input tree

A north-star metric should represent recurring customer value, sit within the product’s influence, and predict sustainable business results. “Monthly active users” is often too broad; “weekly teams completing and sharing a design review” is closer to value. Amplitude recommends pairing the north star with input metrics that explain how teams move it ([Amplitude North Star Playbook](https://info.amplitude.com/rs/138-CDN-550/images/Amplitude-The-North-Star-Playbook.pdf); [Amplitude on choosing a north star](https://amplitude.com/blog/product-north-star-metric)). Mixpanel’s KPI-tree approach links company outcomes to controllable drivers and guardrails ([Mixpanel on KPI trees](https://mixpanel.com/blog/kpi-trees/)).

For a collaboration product:

\[
\text{Weekly valuable workspaces} =
\text{new workspaces}
\times \text{activation rate}
\times \text{week-4 retention}
\times \text{collaboration rate}
\]

Guardrails should include error rate, unsubscribe rate, support contacts, spam reports, unwanted invites, and user-reported trust.

## 7. Step 6 — Build community-led growth around user progress

### Community is not a branded chat room

A community compounds when members gain expertise, reputation, relationships, assets, or economic opportunity by helping one another succeed with the underlying job. Slack or Discord is an interface; the system also needs a purpose, expert supply, rituals, governance, searchable knowledge, and a path between community contribution and product value.

Figma is the strongest B2B SaaS example because community was sequenced with the company’s maturity. Claire Butler’s account describes phases from stealth-era relationships to broad community-led growth and enterprise credibility; the lesson is to cultivate practitioner trust and contribution before attempting to manufacture scale ([First Round on Figma’s five phases](https://review.firstround.com/the-5-phases-of-figmas-community-led-growth-from-stealth-to-enterprise/); [First Round podcast with Claire Butler](https://review.firstround.com/podcast/the-5-phases-of-figmas-community-led-growth-claire-butler/)). Figma Community then turns member supply—files and plugins—into in-product utility and discovery ([Figma Community](https://www.figma.com/community)).

Notion’s template ecosystem similarly gives creators a distribution surface and users a shortcut to outcomes ([Notion Template Gallery](https://www.notion.com/blog/new-notion-template-gallery)). Airtable’s community programs emphasize questions, solutions, product discussion, and member recognition; its navigation guide shows that onboarding and information architecture matter even for the community itself ([Airtable Community guide](https://community.airtable.com/getting-started-on-community-2/navigating-the-airtable-community-46215); [Airtable’s 2024 community highlights](https://community.airtable.com/announcements-6/airtable-community-2024-highlights-1575)).

### Design the community system

**Purpose:** define the job or identity, such as analytics engineering, product operations, collaborative design, or no-code automation. “Users of Product X” is rarely enough.

**Supply:** recruit practitioners who can answer, teach, critique, build templates, and host events. Give early experts direct access, reputation, and product influence.

**Rituals:** office hours, teardown sessions, build challenges, showcases, local meetups, release discussions, and peer onboarding.

**Knowledge:** convert useful conversations into durable, searchable answers and product documentation. Chat-only communities lose compounding value.

**Status:** levels, badges, featured work, speaking opportunities, expert directories, and creator profiles. Status must reflect real contribution, not posting volume.

**Economics:** affiliate revenue, marketplace sales, consulting leads, grants, or credits can strengthen creator supply. Notion’s affiliate program shows one formal way to reward qualified customer acquisition ([Notion affiliate program](https://www.notion.com/affiliates)).

**Governance:** clear moderation, disclosure, safety, self-promotion, and conflict rules. Do not disguise customer support deflection as “community.”

### Measure community as a growth system

Track active contributors, answer rate, time to accepted answer, newcomer-to-contributor conversion, reusable assets created, asset use, community-sourced activation, member retention versus matched non-members, referral exposure, creator earnings, and community-influenced revenue. Avoid claiming causality from the fact that power users join community; use matched cohorts, staggered launches, or invitation experiments where feasible.

## 8. Step 7 — Create an education and creator ecosystem

### Teach the job at three depths

**Discovery education** answers a specific question and captures intent: short videos, examples, glossary pages, and comparison guides.  
**Activation education** gets the user to an outcome: quickstarts, template walkthroughs, migration guides, live workshops, and contextual lessons.  
**Mastery education** builds identity and ecosystem depth: courses, certifications, advanced projects, expert cohorts, and conferences.

Education works in PLG when learning ends in doing. Every tutorial should carry a sample input, expected output, exact product starting point, and next repeated behavior. Canva’s Design School demonstrates the connection between broad education and product-adjacent search demand ([Canva’s content-marketing strategy](https://www.canva.com/learn/content-marketing-strategy/)). HubSpot’s grader pattern demonstrates a complementary path: diagnose first, then teach the user how to improve ([HubSpot Website Grader](https://www.hubspot.com/blog/bid/5539/website-grader-analyzes-over-2-million-sites)).

### Build for creators, not merely influencers

Creators can produce tutorials, templates, plugins, integrations, benchmark datasets, courses, and implementation services. The program should provide:

- A defined audience and content gap.
- Sandbox access and reliable APIs.
- Briefs tied to jobs, not scripted praise.
- Transparent sponsorship and editorial independence.
- Attribution, referral tracking, and marketplace economics.
- Reusable demo data and visual assets.
- Early product access with clear embargoes.
- Quality standards and update obligations.

Template marketplaces create a two-sided loop: more high-quality supply improves time-to-value; more users create audience and economics for suppliers; more suppliers bring users. Andrew Chen’s cold-start analysis applies: seed a dense niche and ensure relevant, high-quality supply before broad expansion ([Andrew Chen’s Cold Start framework](https://andrewchen.com/chapter-one-cold-start/)).

### Certification should validate capability

A weak certificate is a lead-generation badge. A strong one requires demonstrated skill, maps to employment or consulting demand, and creates a trusted expert directory. Measure completion, product activation, certified-user retention, job or client outcomes, employer recognition, and content/referrals generated by graduates. Courses should be free when ecosystem competence expands product demand; charge when instruction itself delivers material professional value.

Build a YouTube portfolio around searchable jobs and proof. Use complete workflows, not feature release videos alone. Track qualified watch time, description-link workflow starts, assisted activation, branded search lift, and creator-code cohorts. Repurpose long tutorials into clips only when each clip retains a coherent job.

## 9. Step 8 — Use paid acquisition as an experiment and accelerator

### Channel roles

Paid media is not disallowed in PLG; it is simply easy to misread because organic, direct, dark social, invites, and product exposure overlap.

- **Google Search:** capture explicit category, problem, comparison, and integration intent. Route each query cluster to the exact workflow or template.
- **LinkedIn:** target roles/accounts for emerging categories, team expansion, customer proof, and PQA air cover. CPMs require downstream account economics, not lead-volume reporting.
- **Meta:** efficient creative testing, retargeting, and broad reach where the user population is large enough; watch low-intent signups.
- **Reddit:** participate around authentic practitioner problems, sponsor high-intent communities carefully, and use job-specific creative. Reddit’s own SaaS guide emphasizes matching creative and landing experiences to the consideration journey ([Reddit’s tech/SaaS advertising guide](https://www.business.reddit.com/smb/guide-to-digital-advertising-for-tech-saas)).

### Test the offer, not just the ad

For each high-intent segment, test:

1. **Freemium CTA:** best when recurring single-player value exists without setup help.
2. **Free-trial CTA:** best when premium capability can be evaluated in a bounded period and urgency helps.
3. **Template/tool CTA:** best when users need proof or a starting asset before adopting.
4. **Interactive demo CTA:** useful when data, security, or setup prevents immediate authentic use.
5. **Book-a-demo CTA:** best for complex migration, governance, or large-account buying—not as the universal default.

Randomize where possible and evaluate activation, retained account, team penetration, paid conversion, gross-margin payback, and sales time—not form conversion. A cheap freemium signup can be economically worse than an expensive activated team.

### Build incrementality into the budget

Attribution asks which touchpoint received credit. Incrementality asks what would not have happened without the spend. Use geo holdouts, audience holdouts, conversion-lift studies, matched markets, or time-based tests with adequate power. Google describes conversion lift as comparing exposed and control groups, while Meta offers conversion-lift testing for causal measurement ([Google Ads Conversion Lift](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/12003020?hl=en); [Meta Conversion Lift](https://www.facebook.com/business/measurement/conversion-lift/)). Google’s broader incrementality guidance recommends experiments that isolate causal impact rather than optimizing solely to attributed conversions ([Think with Google on incrementality](https://www.thinkwithgoogle.com/intl/en-emea/marketing-strategies/data-and-measurement/incrementality-testing/)). LinkedIn’s Brand Lift tests can measure survey-based changes in awareness or consideration when direct conversion is too sparse ([LinkedIn Brand Lift overview](https://www.linkedin.com/help/lms/answer/a419978/brand-lift-testing-overview)).

Hold paid programs to four layers:

- **Platform:** CPM, click-through rate, cost per click.
- **Website/product:** workflow start, signup, activation, TTV.
- **Business:** paid conversion, expansion, gross margin, payback.
- **Causal:** incremental activated accounts and incremental gross profit.

Cap spend until the cohort retains. Paid media should accelerate proven messages, tools, templates, and loops; it should not conceal an activation problem.

## 10. Step 9 — Design freemium, trial, pricing, and packaging economics

### Choose the free experience from the product’s value architecture

**Freemium** works when users can receive durable value individually or in a small team, free users create distribution or ecosystem value, marginal cost is manageable, and a paid boundary emerges naturally through volume, collaboration, governance, or advanced capability.

**Free trial** works when the full value can be experienced in a bounded time, setup is fast, premium functionality is the proof, and a deadline usefully focuses evaluation. A trial fails when users spend most of it configuring or waiting for colleagues.

**Reverse trial** begins with premium capability and falls back to a useful free tier. It can demonstrate differentiation while preserving acquired users, but the downgrade must be clear and non-destructive.

**Usage credit/sandbox** works for infrastructure, data, AI, and developer products where value and cost scale with consumption. The free amount should support an authentic project, not merely an API call.

Kyle Poyar’s benchmark analysis warns against treating all “free-to-paid” rates as comparable: conversion varies materially by freemium versus trial, target customer, denominator, and conversion window ([Growth Unhinged free-to-paid report](https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/free-to-paid-conversion-report)). Lenny Rachitsky’s synthesis similarly separates freemium and free-trial benchmarks and emphasizes definition and context ([Lenny’s free-to-paid conversion guide](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/what-is-a-good-free-to-paid-conversion)). ProductLed notes that opt-in trials, opt-out trials requiring payment details, and freemium create different conversion dynamics and user friction ([ProductLed on free-trial conversion](https://productled.com/blog/saas-free-trial-conversion-rate)).

OpenView’s 2022 product research reported a median 6% visitor-to-signup rate for freemium businesses and roughly 5% of freemium signups converting to paid, while highlighting large variation and the importance of activation. Those figures are diagnostic priors, not universal targets; a company must specify cohort, window, user/account denominator, and plan eligibility ([OpenView 2022 SaaS Benchmarks](https://offers.openviewpartners.com/hubfs/2022%20SaaS%20Benchmarks/OpenView%20SaaS%20Benchmarks%20Report_2022.pdf)).

### Put the paywall after value and before uncontrolled cost

Good paid boundaries align price with increasing customer value:

- More active seats or collaborators.
- More workflows, objects, storage, or history.
- Higher usage or compute.
- Team permissions and administration.
- Security, governance, audit, and compliance.
- Advanced automation, integration, analytics, or support.

Bad boundaries disable the first meaningful outcome, surprise users with irreversible loss, or charge for a capability needed to understand the product. OpenView’s PLG principles explicitly include monetizing after value and, where appropriate, based on usage ([OpenView’s benchmark principles](https://openviewpartners.com/blog/how-do-you-stack-up-2022-saas-benchmarks/)).

Usage-based pricing aligns expansion with consumption but creates bill uncertainty and gross-margin exposure. Use a value metric users can forecast and connect to outcomes; provide meters, alerts, budgets, caps, and calculators. Kyle Poyar’s pricing coverage emphasizes that packaging and monetization need to evolve with the product and its customer value, including AI-related costs that complicate pure seat pricing ([Growth Unhinged on AI monetization](https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/whats-changing-with-ai-monetization); [Growth Unhinged pricing collection](https://www.growthunhinged.com/pricing)). OpenView’s pricing guides recommend treating pricing as an ongoing strategic process rather than a one-time launch decision ([OpenView SaaS Pricing Guide](http://offers.openviewpartners.com/hubfs/ultimate-saas-pricing-guide.pdf); [OpenView on the state of SaaS pricing](https://openviewpartners.com/blog/state-of-saas-pricing/)).

### Price-test responsibly

Use a sequence:

1. Interview by segment about value, alternatives, budget, procurement, and metric predictability.
2. Analyze willingness-to-pay alongside observed usage and cost-to-serve.
3. Prototype package names, boundaries, meters, and upgrade messages.
4. Test messaging and plan comprehension before price levels.
5. Run controlled price or package tests on eligible new cohorts; avoid hidden discrimination.
6. Monitor activation, paid conversion, average revenue, gross margin, support, refund, churn, and expansion.
7. Grandfather or migrate existing users with explicit communication and preserved trust.

Optimize **gross profit per acquired cohort**, not conversion rate alone:

\[
\text{Expected cohort gross profit} =
\text{activated accounts}
\times \text{paid conversion}
\times \text{lifetime gross profit per payer}
- \text{acquisition and service cost}
\]

Report free economics separately: infrastructure/AI cost, support cost, abuse, distribution created, content/template supply, PQL generation, and eventual conversion. A free tier can be economically sound at low direct conversion if it creates high-quality invitations or account expansion; it can be unsound at a superficially attractive conversion rate if marginal cost and churn are high.

## 11. Step 10 — Build the PQL/PQA and sales-assist motion

### Qualify from a value hypothesis

A PQL model should answer three questions:

1. **Fit:** could this user/account become valuable? Use company, role, use case, geography, technical environment, and plan eligibility.
2. **Value:** has the product solved the target job? Use activation, repetition, depth, collaboration, and outcome events.
3. **Buying/expansion signal:** is human help timely? Use limit proximity, rapid seat growth, multiple workspaces, governance/security views, pricing visits, procurement actions, or a request for help.

OpenView defines PQLs through firsthand product behavior and advises using those signals to surface buying intent ([OpenView PQL guide](https://openviewpartners.com/blog/how-to-identify-a-product-qualified-lead)). Kyle Poyar recommends defining PQLs from observed patterns associated with conversion and separating user and account signals ([Growth Unhinged’s PQL guide](https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/your-guide-to-pqls)). ProductLed’s lead-scoring model combines product behavior with customer fit and iterates from real conversion evidence ([ProductLed on PQL lead scoring](https://productled.com/blog/lead-scoring-model-to-uncover-pqls)).

Do not make the model a mysterious 0–100 score. Give sales and lifecycle systems a reason code:

- “Eight active users across two domains; weekly usage doubled.”
- “Admin viewed SSO and audit log after workspace reached free limit.”
- “Activated developer account exceeded production credit and viewed pricing.”
- “High-fit account invited legal/procurement user.”

### Route by the help the user needs

| Signal | Best intervention |
|---|---|
| Activated individual, low complexity | Automated education and upgrade prompt |
| High-fit user stuck before value | Specialist onboarding offer |
| Team spreading organically | Workspace consolidation and admin education |
| Limit reached after recurring value | Contextual upgrade plus optional chat |
| Security/governance interest | Sales-assist with technical proof |
| Multiple teams/domains | Account mapping and expansion |
| Low fit or weak value | No sales outreach; improve product/lifecycle |

Elena Verna’s product-led-sales guidance distinguishes an individual user’s journey from the account’s commercialization and argues that sales should enter where it increases conversion or expansion rather than interrupting every signup ([Elena Verna’s B2B product-led-sales guide](https://www.elenaverna.com/p/elenas-2024-b2b-product-led-sales); [Lenny’s product-led-sales interview](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/the-ultimate-guide-to-product-led)). OpenView’s operational advice similarly treats product-led sales as a distinct capability requiring leadership, process, data, and culture—not simply assigning free users to SDRs ([OpenView on the first steps of product-led sales](https://openviewpartners.com/blog/the-first-steps-of-product-led-sales/)).

### Make outreach product-aware and permission-sensitive

The message should acknowledge an observable need without sounding invasive:

> Your workspace has added several collaborators and is approaching the history limit. Here is a guide to workspace consolidation and permissions. If useful, an implementation specialist can review the setup.

Avoid “I saw you clicked X at 2:14 p.m.” Use account-level context, explain value, and offer an easy opt-out. PostHog’s public handbook reflects a pragmatic product-led-sales approach: prioritize accounts based on product use and potential, while keeping self-serve central ([PostHog product-led sales handbook](https://posthog.com/handbook/growth/sales/product-led-sales)).

### Measure sales assist incrementally

Track PQL/PQA volume, precision, acceptance, speed-to-help, meeting rate, assisted activation, conversion, expansion, sales hours, and user opt-outs. Most importantly, hold out a randomized set of eligible accounts or phase routing across comparable segments. Compare incremental gross profit with the cost of sales time. Sales assist is justified when it creates value that the product and lifecycle system would not have created alone.

## 12. Step 11 — Turn retention and expansion into marketing

### Retention is the permission to grow

Retained users produce references, templates, integrations, public outputs, invitations, reviews, community answers, and expansion. Churned cohorts erase acquisition gains and poison remarketing economics. Amplitude’s benchmark finding—that early activation predicts later retention—supports treating onboarding as the first retention campaign ([Amplitude’s 7% retention rule](https://amplitude.com/blog/7-percent-retention-rule)). Its “7% rule” is a cross-product diagnostic, not a target for every B2B product; cadence should match the natural frequency of value.

Manage retention by the product’s value interval:

- Daily for communication or operational tools.
- Weekly for project, design, or analytics workflows.
- Monthly/quarterly for episodic compliance or planning tools.
- Event-based for scheduling, incident, or transaction products.

Use cohort retention for users **and accounts**. A team can retain while one champion leaves; an individual can remain active while account breadth shrinks. Measure logo retention, user retention, retained seats, retained usage, and net revenue retention.

### Design lifecycle around recurring jobs

Lifecycle programs should prompt value, not generic engagement:

- Resume an unfinished valuable workflow.
- Alert users to a relevant change or anomaly.
- Summarize outcomes achieved.
- Recommend the next template or integration based on the job.
- Bring inactive collaborators back into a shared object.
- Teach an advanced capability after prerequisite value.
- Celebrate team milestones and invite appropriate expansion.

Appcues’ PLG flywheel frames product usage, advocacy, and expansion as reinforcing stages rather than an end-of-funnel conversion ([Appcues on the PLG flywheel](https://www.appcues.com/blog/product-led-growth-flywheel)). Mixpanel’s PLG guidance likewise treats activation, engagement, retention, and monetization as connected behavioral questions ([Mixpanel’s PLG guide](https://mixpanel.com/blog/product-led-growth/)).

### Build explicit referral and advocacy loops

Product-native sharing is the default referral mechanism; add explicit referrals where users can name peers outside the workflow. Trigger the request after a verified success, make the benefit relevant, and reward qualified outcomes. For customer proof:

- Ask after repeated value or a measurable win.
- Offer formats from a short quote to a full case study.
- Preserve editorial approval.
- Capture job, before state, implementation, outcome, and caveat.
- Route proof back to the relevant intent page and onboarding path.

NPS can help segment feedback, but it is not a growth strategy or a substitute for retention. Qualtrics defines NPS from likelihood-to-recommend responses and recommends pairing the score with follow-up feedback ([Qualtrics NPS guide](https://www.qualtrics.com/articles/customer-experience/net-promoter-score/)). Use promoters as a research pool, not an automatic referral audience; ask permission and verify actual advocacy behavior. Close the loop with detractors and “passives,” and relate feedback to product behavior.

### Expansion paths

Expansion should correspond to more value:

- User → repeated user.
- User → collaborator.
- Collaborator group → active team.
- Team → multiple workflows or integrations.
- Team → department/workspace standard.
- Department → governed company deployment.

Team/workspace virality is strongest when the product creates a shared system of record or collaboration object. Andrew Chen’s framework predicts stronger defensibility when network density and relevant activity rise ([*The Cold Start Problem*](https://andrewchen.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/ColdStartProb_9780062969743_AS0928_cc20_Final.pdf)). Measure invitations only as an input; the outcomes are recipient value, multi-user retention, and account expansion.

## 13. Step 12 — Measure the full PLG system

### Use AARRR, but define every stage in value terms

Dave McClure’s AARRR model—acquisition, activation, retention, referral, revenue—remains a useful diagnostic spine ([Startup Metrics for Pirates](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/startup-metrics-4-pirates/2751728?nway-=,)); [Product-Led Alliance’s KPI catalog](https://www.productledalliance.com/23-product-management-metrics-and-kpis/) offers a broader metric inventory. For PLG:

| Stage | Primary question | Core metrics |
|---|---|---|
| Acquisition | Are the right users starting the right job? | Qualified visits, workflow starts, signup by intent/source |
| Activation | Did they experience value quickly? | Activation rate, median/P90 TTV, activation velocity |
| Retention | Do users/accounts repeat value? | Cohort retention, valuable-workspace retention |
| Revenue | Does value become durable gross profit? | Paid conversion, ARPA, gross margin, payback, expansion |
| Referral | Does usage create the next qualified user? | Loop multiplier, cycle time, recipient activation |

Revenue can appear before referral in the acronym without dictating operating priority. Diagnose acquisition cohorts through all downstream stages.

### Build a metric tree

**North star:** weekly valuable workspaces.  
**Acquisition inputs:** qualified organic visits, template/tool starts, recipient visits, paid incremental reach.  
**Activation inputs:** import success, first artifact, integration, collaborator action.  
**Retention inputs:** repeated workflow, multi-user breadth, notification relevance, reliability.  
**Revenue inputs:** eligible active accounts, upgrade exposure, value-metric growth, PQA assist.  
**Referral inputs:** share rate, recipients per share, recipient activation, creator supply.

Amplitude’s North Star framework recommends a value-representative metric with controllable inputs; Mixpanel’s KPI-tree guidance helps connect inputs to business outcomes ([Amplitude North Star Playbook](https://info.amplitude.com/rs/138-CDN-550/images/Amplitude-The-North-Star-Playbook.pdf); [Mixpanel KPI trees](https://mixpanel.com/blog/kpi-trees/)).

### Required dashboards

1. **Journey cohort:** source → signup → activation → week-4 retained → paid → expansion.
2. **Activation path:** completion and elapsed time for each critical step, segmented by job.
3. **Loop dashboard:** exposures, shares/invites, recipient activation, reproduction, cycle time.
4. **SEO asset portfolio:** page type → workflow start → activated/retained account → revenue.
5. **Free economics:** cost-to-serve, conversion, PQA yield, distribution created.
6. **PQA/sales assist:** eligibility, routing, incremental lift, sales cost, user experience.
7. **Experiment ledger:** hypothesis, primary metric, guardrails, sample, result, decision.

### Cohort discipline and data contracts

Define user, account, active, activated, retained, paid, expansion, invite, and PQL in a shared metric dictionary. Instrument server-side outcomes where feasible; preserve event version, identity merge, source, and experiment exposure. Audit missing events and bot/internal traffic. Heap’s research notes continuing difficulty turning fragmented behavioral data into action, reinforcing the need for governed definitions and integrated workflows rather than more dashboards ([Heap Digital Insights report](https://www.heap.io/press/heap-digital-insights-report-reveals-data-stack-challenges)).

### Attribution, incrementality, and MMM

Use attribution for routing and creative optimization, experiments for causality, and marketing-mix modeling (MMM) for aggregate allocation once spend, time variation, and conversion volume are sufficient. PLG complicates identity because a recipient may see a shared artifact on one device, search later, join a colleague’s workspace, and pay at the account level. Maintain both user- and account-level paths, tag product exposures, and accept that deterministic attribution will remain incomplete.

MMM should include paid spend, brand/search demand, seasonality, pricing changes, product launches, invitation volume, template/tool launches, sales-assist changes, and major reliability events. Do not deploy MMM as ceremonial precision for a small company with little channel variation. Run geo or audience experiments to calibrate model priors; Google and Meta’s lift-testing frameworks provide practical causal designs ([Think with Google incrementality guide](https://www.thinkwithgoogle.com/intl/en-emea/marketing-strategies/data-and-measurement/incrementality-testing/); [Meta Conversion Lift](https://www.facebook.com/business/measurement/conversion-lift/)).

### Executive scorecard

Report no more than:

- North-star level and growth.
- New activated accounts and activation rate.
- Median/P90 time-to-value.
- Week-4 or use-frequency-adjusted account retention.
- Natural/product-driven share of new activated accounts.
- Loop multiplier for the primary loop.
- Free-to-paid conversion by product experience and window.
- Net revenue retention and gross margin.
- Incremental CAC/payback by scalable channel.
- PQA-assisted incremental gross profit.

## 14. Common anti-patterns

### 1. Bolting SDRs onto every signup

Why it fails: it turns product interest into interruption, overloads reps with weak signals, and prevents learning which behavior predicts value.  
Correction: define PQA/PQL reasons, route by need, cap outreach, and use holdouts. ProductLed warns that PQL models fail when behavior is reduced to simplistic activity counts ([ProductLed on what PQLs get wrong](https://productled.com/blog/pqls)).

### 2. Calling a free plan “PLG”

Why it fails: free access without discovery, fast value, retention, sharing, and monetization is a cost center.  
Correction: map the full loop and free-tier economics before launch. Elena Verna argues that not every product or market is suited to PLG; complexity, user, and value delivery matter ([Elena Verna, “To PLG or not PLG?”](https://www.elenaverna.com/p/to-plg-or-not-plg)).

### 3. Optimizing signup conversion before activation

Why it fails: aggressive forms, offers, or low-intent campaigns inflate the denominator and hide weak value.  
Correction: optimize cost per activated and retained account; make activation a shared marketing/product goal.

### 4. Hiding pricing

Why it fails: self-serve buyers cannot qualify themselves, comparison intent leaks to third parties, and trust erodes.  
Correction: show plan logic, value metric, limits, examples, and enterprise contact conditions. If exact enterprise price depends on scope, still disclose the pricing architecture.

### 5. Gating everything

Why it fails: the company exchanges reach and trust for low-information emails, while AI/search summaries reduce the value of generic PDFs.  
Correction: ungate education and one-time utility; require identity only to save, personalize, collaborate, or secure outputs.

### 6. Programmatic SEO without unique value

Why it fails: thousands of thin combinations create index risk and no product bridge.  
Correction: require unique data, a real job, maintained supply, and an executable workflow. Zapier’s programmatic SEO guide stresses quality and user value over automated page volume ([Zapier on programmatic SEO](https://zapier.com/blog/programmatic-seo/)).

### 7. Ignoring the SEO/free-tool moat

Why it fails: competitors accumulate links, intent data, templates, creator supply, and returning users while the company rents demand through ads.  
Correction: treat utilities, templates, integrations, and directories as product investments with engineering ownership.

### 8. A generic onboarding tour

Why it fails: describing navigation does not complete the user’s job.  
Correction: segment by intent, pre-populate the starting state, and guide to a real output. Appcues recommends journey-based, contextual onboarding ([Appcues product-led onboarding](https://www.appcues.com/blog/product-led-onboarding)).

### 9. Treating community as support deflection

Why it fails: unanswered questions and vendor promotion exhaust members.  
Correction: fund expert supply, durable knowledge, moderation, status, and member outcomes.

### 10. Confusing invitations with virality

Why it fails: spammy invites may increase sends while reducing recipient activation and trust.  
Correction: measure the complete loop through recipient value and reproduction.

### 11. Copying conversion benchmarks without definitions

Why it fails: freemium, opt-in trial, opt-out trial, user, account, and time window produce incompatible rates.  
Correction: publish cohort definitions next to every benchmark. Growth Unhinged’s conversion report explicitly segments the free experience and denominator ([Growth Unhinged free-to-paid benchmarks](https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/free-to-paid-conversion-report)).

### 12. Scaling paid media on attributed signups

Why it fails: retargeting and brand search harvest users generated by organic/product loops, while cheap signups may not retain.  
Correction: use lift tests and downstream cohort economics.

### 13. A north star with no customer value

Why it fails: logins, clicks, and messages can grow through notifications or friction.  
Correction: count completed recurring value, then attach input and quality metrics.

### 14. Premature enterprise mimicry

Why it fails: a large sales team, bespoke demos, hidden pricing, and gated content can smother the self-serve signal before product-market fit.  
Correction: keep enterprise sales as a complementary path for complexity and expansion; do not make it the front door for everyone.

## 15. The first 90 days for a PLG marketing leader

### Operating principles

- Start with customer value and retention, not a campaign calendar.
- Establish one source of metric truth.
- Diagnose by job, segment, source, and cohort.
- Fix the largest constraint before adding acquisition.
- Ship a few durable assets and loops, not dozens of disconnected tactics.
- Pair every growth metric with a trust or quality guardrail.

### Days 1–30: Diagnose and align

**Customer and market**

- Interview 10–15 activated/retained users, 5–10 churned or stalled users, and several recent paid converters. Reconstruct trigger, job, workaround, setup, aha moment, anxiety, and buying event.
- Review search queries, sales calls, support conversations, reviews, community questions, cancellations, and upgrade feedback.
- Map the primary job and one or two secondary jobs; identify the self-serve ICP and where enterprise assistance is genuinely needed.

**Data**

- Audit identity resolution, source tracking, critical events, workspace/account model, activation definition, retention cadence, pricing eligibility, and experiment exposure.
- Rebuild the journey cohort from landing page through week-4 retention and payment.
- Define activation candidates and validate them against retention.
- Establish metric contracts and baseline confidence intervals.

**Growth system**

- Inventory SEO page types, tools, templates, integrations, public outputs, invites, lifecycle messages, community, creator relationships, and paid campaigns.
- Diagram the primary loop with multiplier and cycle time.
- Quantify the biggest leak: wrong acquisition, setup failure, slow TTV, weak recurring value, poor collaboration, or monetization.
- Pause campaigns optimized to low-quality signups when downstream data is absent.

**Outputs by day 30**

1. One-page PLG growth model.
2. Jobs/message matrix and intent map.
3. Activation definition with evidence and version.
4. Baseline AARRR cohort dashboard.
5. Primary-loop model.
6. Free-tier unit-economics model.
7. Prioritized constraint backlog.

Lenny’s PLG guidance recommends establishing a focused team, defining the PLG opportunity, and sequencing the motion rather than launching a free experience without foundations ([Lenny, “Five steps to starting your PLG motion”](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/five-steps-to-starting-your-plg-motion); [Lenny’s guide to adding PLG](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/the-ultimate-guide-to-adding-a-plg)).

### Days 31–60: Fix activation and build one compounding asset

**Activation sprint**

- Remove or defer nonessential setup.
- Align the top acquisition page/template with the in-product starting state.
- Add a completed example or sample data with a path to real data.
- Fix the largest error/import/integration failure.
- Launch one behavior-triggered lifecycle sequence with a holdout.
- Offer human help only to high-fit stalled accounts.

**Acquisition asset**

Choose one based on evidence:

- High-intent template collection.
- Free diagnostic/calculator/converter.
- Integration directory page type.
- Comparison/alternative cluster.
- Creator-led practical course.

Build a product bridge, quality standard, ownership, instrumentation, and refresh process. Do not publish 1,000 pages before 10 prove useful.

**Loop sprint**

- Improve recipient landing and first action for the strongest share/invite.
- Make attribution respectful and removable where appropriate.
- Measure full-loop conversion and cycle time.
- Run one experiment at the constraint step.

**PQA prototype**

- Create transparent fit, value, and expansion rules.
- Generate reason codes.
- Route a small eligible cohort to trained sales-assist or customer success.
- Maintain a control group.

**Outputs by day 60**

1. Shortened critical path and TTV dashboard.
2. Activation experiment results.
3. First durable SEO/tool/template asset live.
4. Primary-loop experiment live.
5. PQA pilot and lifecycle reason codes.
6. Revised paid-media landing/CTA test.

### Days 61–90: Prove, scale, and institutionalize

**Prove quality**

- Read early cohorts through activation and the first meaningful retention interval.
- Validate that the new asset attracts the intended job and produces retained accounts.
- Estimate incremental lift for lifecycle, paid, and sales-assist pilots.
- Review guardrails: support burden, invite complaints, unsubscribe, latency, error, and free cost.

**Scale what worked**

- Expand the winning page/template/tool type to a carefully governed second batch.
- Recruit initial expert creators or community contributors around the successful job.
- Build internal linking, distribution, and creator co-marketing.
- Increase paid budget only where retained cohort economics and incrementality support it.
- Refine packaging/upgrade messages from observed value and objections.

**Institutionalize**

- Create a weekly growth review focused on constraint, experiment evidence, and cohort quality.
- Assign owners for activation, loop, SEO asset supply, community/creator supply, PQA routing, and monetization.
- Publish a metric dictionary, experiment archive, and page-type quality standard.
- Agree with sales on PLG-to-enterprise handoff, suppression, service-level expectations, and feedback.
- Plan the next S-curve only after the current loop is measurable; Casey Winters’ sequencing framework warns against assuming one growth engine compounds forever ([Casey Winters on product sequencing](https://caseyaccidental.com/s-curves-and-product-sequencing/)).

**Outputs by day 90**

1. Executive scorecard with cohort quality.
2. Evidence-backed annual PLG growth model.
3. Two-quarter roadmap across activation, loop, intent capture, ecosystem, and monetization.
4. Paid incrementality test plan.
5. Pricing/packaging research plan.
6. Scaled PQA rules with an incremental ROI threshold.
7. Community/creator charter tied to member outcomes.

### Suggested 90-day targets

Set targets only after the baseline, but use directional commitments:

- Improve activation at the primary bottleneck.
- Reduce median and P90 TTV.
- Raise retained-workspace creation from the top two organic intents.
- Improve one full-loop step without harming recipient trust.
- Launch one durable utility/template system with measurable product continuation.
- Establish a PQA pilot that demonstrates incremental—not attributed—gross profit.
- Move paid reporting from signup CPA to retained-account economics.

## Final doctrine

PLG marketing wins when it makes the product easier to discover, faster to value, more useful with others, and more credible through users and experts. The durable assets are not campaign lists; they are activation paths, search utilities, templates, creator supply, shared outputs, integrations, behavioral qualification, and measurement systems. Marketing still builds category understanding and demand, but it must connect every promise to an executable product experience.

The order matters:

1. Find a valuable, frequent job and express it clearly.
2. Deliver that value quickly.
3. Prove users and accounts repeat it.
4. Turn use into distribution and ecosystem supply.
5. Monetize after value with transparent packaging.
6. Add sales assist where human help creates incremental expansion.
7. Scale acquisition only with retained cohort economics.

That is the boundary with the enterprise sales-led companion: enterprise selling handles organizational complexity after product evidence exists. PLG marketing’s mandate is to make value discoverable, experiential, repeatable, shareable, and measurable before that handoff.
