# Marketing a CRO, Biotech, or Medtech to Buyers Who Need Evidence, Not More Marketing

## What you'll take away

- Why scientific and healthcare buyers are skeptical of generic marketing
- What an internal champion needs to move a complex purchase forward
- Why a better evidence package matters more than another gated white paper

---

Your buyer is not "the healthcare market."

Your buyer is a principal investigator, a medical director, a lab manager, a finance lead, an operations leader, a compliance reviewer, or the internal champion trying to align all of them.

They are not allergic to marketing because it is marketing. They are skeptical of content that asks for attention before it offers evidence.

When the purchase carries clinical, scientific, financial, regulatory, or implementation risk, buyers look for material they can use. They read protocols, technical specifications, published evidence, workflow requirements, pricing assumptions, security documentation, and peer references.

If your best early asset is a gated PDF called *The Complete Guide to Transforming Precision Medicine*, a serious buyer may assume it contains broad claims instead of the information needed to make a decision.

The job is not to stop marketing.

The job is to make marketing useful in a real buying process.

## Who is actually in the room

Complex B2B decisions are usually cross functional. [Forrester's 2025 buyer survey](https://www.forrester.com/blogs/three-realities-about-b2b-buying-networks/), written up in February 2026, reports an average of 13 people inside the buyer's organization and nine from outside it. A [Gartner sales survey published in May 2025](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-05-07-gartner-sales-survey-finds-74-percent-of-b2b-buyer-teams-demonstrate-unhealthy-conflict-during-the-decision-process), of 632 B2B buyers, found that 74% of B2B buyer teams demonstrate unhealthy conflict during the decision. Both are broad B2B samples. They are not a headcount of the people in your hospital, your lab, or your value analysis meeting.

The exact group varies by product and institution. A high consideration healthcare or life science purchase commonly involves:

- A clinical or scientific evaluator asking, "Does the evidence support this?"
- A financial stakeholder asking, "What will this actually cost?"
- An operations lead asking, "What will this require from my team?"
- A regulatory, privacy, security, or compliance reviewer asking, "What risk are we taking on?"
- An end user asking, "Will this work in the real setting?"
- An internal champion asking, "Can I make a defensible case for this?"

That last person matters most.

The champion is not necessarily the only person reading your materials. They are often the person responsible for circulating them, explaining them, and answering the first round of objections.

You are not only writing to convince a prospect. You are equipping someone to make the case when you are not in the room.

## What they use instead

Many B2B buyers form preferences before a formal sales conversation. The exact percentage differs by study. The pattern is consistent: buyers research on their own, build a short list, and often start with a front runner before a seller is involved.

The assets that help you make that short list are rarely generic brand stories. They are materials a buyer can use:

- A clear methods or technical note
- A published study, a validation summary, or another credible evidence base
- A case study with context, an outcome, and the limitations
- An implementation overview that explains time, staffing, training, and dependencies
- A plain explanation of price, total cost, or budget impact
- A technical documentation library
- A peer institution reference or a relevant customer example

Every one of those is marketing.

None of them has to look like marketing.

## The value analysis test

For many hospital and health system purchases, a value analysis committee, or a comparable internal review, is a major gate. It may look at clinical fit, safety, financial impact, workflow, implementation, and the evidence behind the request. Requirements vary by system. The buyer usually needs some combination of clinical data, specifications, cost information, comparisons, training plans, and peer references.

Your materials should make that work easier.

If a champion needs three sales calls to get a technical spec, a pricing assumption, or a clinical reference, you have made it harder for the person trying to advocate for you.

If the evidence is buried behind a form fill or hidden in a brand heavy brochure, it becomes harder to circulate internally.

A useful question is: could someone inside this account find, understand, and share the evidence for your product without chasing you for a week?

If the answer is no, the early material is not doing its job.

## The datasheet problem

The problem is not that a datasheet is 12 pages long.

The problem is when page one contains brand language, page two contains unsupported claims, and the technical information appears only after the buyer has worked to find it.

A clinical evaluator looks for methods. Finance looks for assumptions. Operations looks for implementation. Compliance looks for status, risk, privacy, or security information.

If each stakeholder has to dig for what matters, they may stop reading, or they may choose the vendor whose materials make the decision easier.

Keep the full technical documentation if buyers need it. Pair it with a concise, evidence first decision sheet:

- What it does
- Who it is for
- What evidence supports it
- What implementation requires
- What it costs, or how to evaluate cost
- Where it fits, and where it does not

That is more credible than a product matrix in which every competitor column is gray and yours is all green.

## Stop treating lead volume as proof

For long, committee led sales cycles, lead velocity is a weak primary metric.

A new lead may take months to become a qualified opportunity, move through technical or clinical review, reach procurement or a hospital committee, and close only after the reporting period in which marketing was judged.

Track leads if they help you see whether buyers are showing up. Also track the signals that show whether an account is moving toward a real decision:

- Identified, credible internal champions
- Use of technical and evidence assets
- Meetings with the appropriate stakeholders
- Progression through review stages
- Time stalled in each stage
- Conversion from qualified account to opportunity to closed business

The short version: judge a long cycle on movement toward consensus and purchase, not only on form fills.

## A one week reset

You cannot create missing clinical evidence or resolve an unclear regulatory path in a week.

You can identify what is keeping a buyer from using your existing materials.

This week:

1. Open your three best performing marketing assets.
2. Highlight every important claim without a citation, method, assumption, or limitation.
3. Choose the two pieces with the most credible technical content.
4. Make them easy to access and share at stable URLs, where appropriate.
5. Add a short decision use summary at the top: what this supports, what evidence sits behind it, and what a buyer should know about fit and limitations.
6. Separate the polished brochure from the technical material. Keep both, but do not make buyers dig through one to get the other.

That is the reset.

## The honest limit

Marketing can organize evidence and make it easier to use.

It cannot create evidence that does not exist.

If your clinical data is thin, your implementation story is unclear, your security documentation is incomplete, or your regulatory position is unresolved, the next work belongs with the scientific, product, regulatory, quality, and operations teams.

If the product is ready, the marketing job is simple. Make the case easy to evaluate. Make the evidence easy to find. Make it easy for the champion to carry your materials into the room where the decision gets made.
