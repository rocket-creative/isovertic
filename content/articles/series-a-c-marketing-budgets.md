# Series A to C Marketing Budgets: What's Defensible When You're Not Yet Cash-Flow Positive

## What you'll take away

- What the 2026 software spending research actually says about marketing budgets
- How to use benchmarks without treating a funding round as a spending prescription
- What customer acquisition cost payback measures, and how to compare your company with relevant peers
- Illustrative marketing budgets at $2 million, $5 million, and $10 million in annual recurring revenue
- Board slide language that separates spending, acquisition efficiency, and runway

---

Your board wants to know why your marketing spend is what it is. Behind the polite question is a more direct one: does this investment make sense while you are still burning cash?

That is a fair question. A benchmark helps answer it. Being inside a published range is not enough.

A defensible budget connects four things: the work you need to fund, the customers that work can realistically acquire, the time it takes to recover acquisition costs, and the cash available to support that plan.

This framework is for recurring revenue software businesses, including healthcare and life sciences SaaS. The benchmarks below should not be applied directly to therapeutics developers, medical device manufacturers, or other companies with materially different revenue models. The source populations are private B2B SaaS companies ([SaaS Capital, 2026 spending benchmarks](https://www.saas-capital.com/blog-posts/spending-benchmarks-for-private-b2b-saas-companies/)) and SaaS or AI native software companies ([Aleph, CAC payback, 2026](https://www.getaleph.com/answers/cac-payback-period-saas-2026)).

## What SaaS companies spend on marketing in 2026

[SaaS Capital's 2026 spending survey](https://www.saas-capital.com/blog-posts/spending-benchmarks-for-private-b2b-saas-companies/) included more than 1,000 private B2B SaaS companies. It reports median marketing spending of 8% of annual recurring revenue, unchanged from the previous year. Selling costs are reported separately, with a median of 15% of annual recurring revenue.

That distinction matters. An 8% marketing benchmark is not an 8% sales and marketing benchmark.

It is also an overall median, not a Series A, B, or C spending requirement. SaaS Capital reports differences by funding status and revenue scale. Equity backed companies spend more on marketing than bootstrapped companies, and the report provides separate comparisons by annual recurring revenue.

Use the median as a reference point, then ask whether the comparison fits:

- Is the source measuring marketing alone, or sales and marketing together?
- Does the peer group resemble your revenue scale and funding model?
- Are people, agencies, media, events, and software included in your spending figure?
- Are you building a marketing function, or operating one that already exists?
- Is the figure an annual spending rate, a trailing annual total, or one quarter's expense?

A funding round does not answer those questions. Two Series A companies can have different revenue, gross margins, sales motions, and amounts of work still to build.

If your budget exceeds the overall median, explain the difference through the operating plan. Do not rely on "we are Series A" or "we sell into healthcare" as the entire justification.

## What payback tells you, and what it does not

Customer acquisition cost payback, usually shortened to CAC payback, estimates how many months of gross profit it takes to recover the cost of acquiring customers.

It is an acquisition efficiency measure, not a marketing only performance score. Its cost base generally includes sales and marketing, while the spending benchmark above measures marketing separately ([SaaS Capital, 2026](https://www.saas-capital.com/blog-posts/spending-benchmarks-for-private-b2b-saas-companies/), [Aleph, 2026](https://www.getaleph.com/answers/cac-payback-period-saas-2026)).

Aleph's 2026 summary of the Aleph and Benchmarkit research reports median CAC payback of 16 months using full year 2025 results. The overall study included 342 SaaS and AI native software companies. The payback figures came from the 198 participants that reported that metric ([Aleph, CAC payback, 2026](https://www.getaleph.com/answers/cac-payback-period-saas-2026)).

The relevant comparison changes with the business:

- Companies with annual contract values below $5,000 had a median payback of 11 months.
- Companies with annual contract values from $50,000 to $100,000 had a median payback of 22 months.
- Vertical SaaS companies had a median payback of 18 months, compared with 14 months for horizontal B2B SaaS.

Those differences are why a headline median should start the discussion, not settle it.

A 22 month payback period means something different for a low price, self service product than it does for an enterprise product with large contracts and strong retention. Review payback alongside gross margin, retention, contract value, and available cash.

### Calculate it consistently

For a new customer acquisition view, use this: CAC payback in months equals customer acquisition cost divided by monthly gross profit from those customers.

If you use an aggregate calculation, align the acquisition spending with the customers and new recurring revenue it helped produce. Apply gross margin rather than treating all revenue as available to repay acquisition costs.

Aleph's published formula is prior period sales and marketing expense, divided by the product of new annual recurring revenue and gross margin, then multiplied by 12. It specifically warns that mismatching the timing of spending and revenue can distort the result ([Aleph, CAC payback, 2026](https://www.getaleph.com/answers/cac-payback-period-saas-2026)).

Document what your calculation includes. If one period uses all sales and marketing expense while another uses only paid media and agency fees, the apparent improvement is not a meaningful comparison.

### Estimated payback is not observed recovery

You do not need to wait until customers have fully repaid their acquisition costs before estimating CAC payback.

An estimate uses acquisition costs and the gross profit generated by the acquired customers. Observed recovery follows those customers over time to see whether the expected repayment actually happens.

For board reporting, distinguish the two:

- Estimated CAC payback, including the calculation and timing assumptions
- Observed cohort performance, including retention and gross profit to date
- Cohort maturity, including whether enough customers have closed to make the estimate useful

That is more informative than assigning every business an 18 to 24 month waiting period before it can report an acquisition efficiency metric. The appropriate timing depends on the actual sales motion and the data available.

## Sales cycle length sets the review schedule

Use your own sales cycle data to decide when to expect closed revenue from a marketing investment.

For healthcare and life sciences software, examine where time is actually spent. Your review may need to account for technical evaluation, security review, procurement, contracting, or a pilot. Do not substitute a generic industry average for the process your customers follow.

For this operating framework, use three review levels.

### Monthly: execution and early movement

Review whether the planned work happened and whether qualified prospects are responding.

Useful measures may include:

- Qualified inquiries from target accounts
- Response time and follow up completion
- Inquiry to meeting conversion
- Sales acceptance of marketing generated opportunities
- Progress on priority content, campaigns, and website improvements

Choose the measures that connect to your sales process. Rankings and traffic can help diagnose performance. Do not present them as proof of future revenue.

### Quarterly: opportunity development and spending

Review spending against plan, opportunities created, stage progression, losses, and changes in the forecast.

For immature cohorts, report what is known and what remains uncertain. Do not imply that an open opportunity has already delivered a return.

### As cohorts mature: acquisition economics

Update CAC payback and observed performance as customers close and generate gross profit. Align spending with the relevant acquisition period and compare similar customer segments.

A long sales cycle does not mean suspending review. It means distinguishing early evidence from results that require more time.

## What a budget could look like at $2 million, $5 million, and $10 million

The following budgets are illustrative operating plans, not survey findings or vendor price quotes. They show how a company might allocate a selected spending level.

The examples use single amounts so the line items reconcile with the total. They do not establish what a company should spend merely because it has reached a particular revenue level or funding round.

Use a consistent expense definition. Here, the people and specialists line includes fully loaded marketing personnel costs and retained specialists. Other lines represent additional costs not already included there. Event costs are averaged across the year for planning, although cash payments may occur earlier.

### $2 million: an illustrative early stage budget

Annual marketing budget: $300,000. Monthly planning average: $25,000. Marketing spending rate: 15% of annual recurring revenue.

| Line item | Monthly planning amount |
| --- | --- |
| Marketing personnel and retained specialists | $13,000 |
| Paid media | $5,000 |
| Additional technical content production | $3,500 |
| CRM, automation, and analytics | $2,000 |
| Conferences and events, averaged across the year | $1,500 |
| Total | $25,000 |

This example sits above SaaS Capital's overall 8% median. The board explanation therefore needs to identify what the additional investment funds and how the company will evaluate it. The overall median is context, not evidence that this particular budget is right or wrong ([SaaS Capital, 2026](https://www.saas-capital.com/blog-posts/spending-benchmarks-for-private-b2b-saas-companies/)).

Suggested framing: "Our annual marketing plan is $300,000, or 15% of current annual recurring revenue. This is above the overall survey median. The difference funds [specific buildout or programs]. We will review qualified opportunity creation monthly, spending and stage progression quarterly, and acquisition economics as the relevant cohorts mature."

### $5 million: an illustrative scaling budget

Annual marketing budget: $660,000. Monthly planning average: $55,000. Marketing spending rate: 13.2% of annual recurring revenue.

| Line item | Monthly planning amount |
| --- | --- |
| Marketing personnel and retained specialists | $25,000 |
| Paid media | $13,000 |
| Additional content and technical documentation | $8,000 |
| Conferences and events, averaged across the year | $4,000 |
| Tools and technology | $3,500 |
| Additional specialist review and compliance support, where needed | $1,500 |
| Total | $55,000 |

SaaS Capital's published chart for a typical company in the $3 million to $5 million annual recurring revenue band also shows 8% median marketing spend, the same as the overall sample ([SaaS Capital, 2026](https://www.saas-capital.com/blog-posts/spending-benchmarks-for-private-b2b-saas-companies/)). A company choosing 13.2% should explain why its plan warrants spending above that reference point.

At this level, the proposal should identify which programs have evidence behind them, which remain experiments, and what would trigger a change in allocation.

Suggested framing: "Our annual marketing plan is $660,000, or 13.2% of annual recurring revenue. We are allocating $[X] to programs with demonstrated results and $[Y] to defined experiments. Our estimated payback is [N] months using [method and acquisition period]. The next allocation review is [date], based on [specific measures]."

### $10 million: an illustrative larger operating budget

Annual marketing budget: $1.2 million. Monthly planning average: $100,000. Marketing spending rate: 12% of annual recurring revenue.

| Line item | Monthly planning amount |
| --- | --- |
| Marketing personnel and retained specialists | $45,000 |
| Paid media | $24,000 |
| Additional content, technical documentation, and account programs | $13,000 |
| Conferences, events, and field marketing | $9,000 |
| Tools and technology | $6,000 |
| Additional specialist review and compliance support, where needed | $3,000 |
| Total | $100,000 |

At this scale, break out acquisition economics by the segments that matter to your business rather than relying exclusively on a blended number. Aleph's results show why: contract value and sales motion materially change the relevant payback comparison ([Aleph, 2026](https://www.getaleph.com/answers/cac-payback-period-saas-2026)).

Suggested framing: "Our annual marketing plan is $1.2 million, or 12% of annual recurring revenue. Acquisition efficiency varies by [segment or sales motion], so we report those groups separately. We are increasing investment in [program] based on [evidence] and limiting [program] until [decision criterion] is met."

None of these budgets should be approved on percentage alone. Each needs an operating plan and a cash forecast that support it.

## The board slide language

For the next board deck, use one slide with three sections.

### Spending and the comparison basis

"Our approved annual marketing budget is $[A], or [B]% of current annual recurring revenue. Actual marketing spending in the quarter was $[C], equivalent to an annualized spending rate of $[C times 4], or [D]% of current annual recurring revenue. SaaS Capital's 2026 survey reports an overall median of 8%. Our spending differs because [specific operating reasons]."

Multiplying a quarter by four produces an annualized rate, not a forecast. If the quarter includes unusual event costs or one time projects, explain that or use a more representative period.

Do not divide one quarter's marketing expense by annual recurring revenue and compare the result directly with an annual spending benchmark.

### Acquisition efficiency and cohort maturity

"Estimated payback is [N] months for [segment or acquisition cohort], using [cost definition], [spending lag], and [gross margin]. The 2026 Aleph and Benchmarkit research reports a 16 month overall median using 2025 results, with differences by contract value and sales motion. Our selected comparison is [peer group]. The current cohort is [maturity status], and observed performance to date is [result]."

The payback sample in that study contains 198 respondents, not all 342 companies in the overall research ([Aleph, 2026](https://www.getaleph.com/answers/cac-payback-period-saas-2026)).

### Decisions and cash implications

"We propose to maintain, increase, or reduce [program] because [evidence]. The next review is [date], using [decision criteria]. Under the current company cash forecast, this plan supports runway through [date]. The downside case assumes [slower sales, lower conversion, or another relevant risk]."

That gives the board a spending basis, a relevant efficiency measure, and a decision. It also makes the uncertainty visible.

## The honest limit

CAC payback does not tell you when the whole company becomes cash flow positive. It addresses recovery of acquisition costs, not every operating expense. SaaS Capital reports research and development, general and administrative costs, customer support, and other spending separately from sales and marketing ([SaaS Capital, 2026](https://www.saas-capital.com/blog-posts/spending-benchmarks-for-private-b2b-saas-companies/)).

A budget inside a published range can still be unaffordable. A budget above a median can be reasonable if the company has a credible plan, evidence supporting the investment, and enough cash to carry the risk.

If you have not found a repeatable acquisition motion, present the budget as an experiment plan. State what you are testing, what success looks like, what it costs, and when you will stop or change course.

If runway is tight, model the cash consequences of slower results before approving more spend. A spending benchmark does not extend the time available to make the plan work.

The defensible answer is not simply "other companies spend this much." It is "this is what we are funding, this is the evidence, and this is what we will do if the results differ from the plan."
